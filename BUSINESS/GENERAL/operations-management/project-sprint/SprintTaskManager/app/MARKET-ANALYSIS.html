1What the tool actually is

Under the hood, this is a sprint-first team task manager: work is organized into time-boxed periods (sprints), each with a written goal, and a shared team moves task cards across a kanban board until the period closes. The engine is entirely general-purpose; only the current seed data (a small nonprofit-style team) is specific.

Core engine capabilities

  • Sprints with name, start/end dates, goal and status (planned / active), with smart defaults that chain the next sprint after the previous one
  • Kanban board with four statuses - To Do, In Progress, Done, Blocked - and drag-and-drop card movement
  • Task fields: title, description, priority (critical/high/medium/low), assignee, due date, notes
  • Labels with color coding, file attachments and external links per task
  • List view with sortable columns (priority, title, status, assignee, due date, sprint)
  • Report view: completion donut chart, workload bars by priority, overdue list, recent activity, completed-in-sprint list
  • Team view from the CMS permitted-users list, with manager/staff role badges
  • Manager/staff permission model: managers own sprints, staff only move their own tasks
  • Search plus assignee/priority/status filters
  • PDF and CSV export of the current sprint

What it is not

  • Not a project manager: no Gantt charts, dependencies, subtasks or resource planning
  • Not a recurring-obligation tracker: that is the RecurringTaskManager sibling (daily/weekly/monthly cadences, per-occurrence evidence)
  • Not a scrum tool: no story points, velocity charts, burndown or ceremonies
  • Not a document store: attachments and links live on tasks, but the task is the first-class object
The defining feature: a team works in time-boxed cycles with a written goal, and every task belongs to one cycle. Completion math, overdue pressure and assignee accountability are computed per sprint - which is what turns a board into a management tool.

2Why rename it - the naming question

The working name PersonalTaskManager is misleading on two counts: the tool is built for teams (assignees, permitted users, manager/staff roles, team view), and it is organized around sprints, not a personal to-do list. "Personal" undersells it and signals the wrong market.

Candidate names, assessed

Candidate Verdict Reasoning
SprintTaskManager Pick States the differentiator (sprints) and the category (task manager). Consistent with the sibling names RecurringTaskManager and HierarchyItemChecklistManager in the TASK_MANAGERS folder. Clear, searchable, honest.
TeamSprintManager Alt Adds the team aspect but hides the word "task"; slightly longer and reads more like an app than a category.
SprintBoard / SprintFlow No Brandable but vague; does not follow the folder's naming convention and says nothing about what it manages.
PersonalTaskManager (keep) No Mislabels a multi-user team tool as a personal one; blocks the B2B story from the first impression.
Recommendation: rename to SprintTaskManager. File names, tool title, folder name and documentation should follow (e.g. SprintTaskManager.html/.css/.js). The CMS type names (taskSprints-uniconbaseapps, taskItems-uniconbaseapps) are technical identifiers and can stay unchanged.

3Today's user: the small structured team

The current instance serves a small team in a nonprofit-style organization (the workspace shows Canadian-centre IDs and the developer subdomain centrecanada). Its usage pattern: a manager creates a two-week sprint with a goal, assigns tasks to team members, and the team moves cards on the board while the manager watches the report view.

That profile is the first beachhead, but it is one instance of a much larger pattern: any team of roughly 2-15 people that delivers work in short cycles toward a stated goal, and needs a shared answer to "what are we doing this period, who owns it, and are we on track?"

6Customer types and how it could be sold

  • Small-team subscriptions (B2B): agencies, clinics, stores and crews pay per workspace or per seat. They buy outcomes: sprints that close, overloads that surface, meetings that shorten.
  • Vertical editions: the same engine, re-skinned and re-seeded - Campaign edition (agencies), Fundraiser edition (nonprofits), Store-Ops edition (retail). The four kanban columns and fields stay; only seed labels and demo content change.
  • Platform-embedded (B2B2C): in the UniconHub context it sells as one CMS tool among many: "the team-cycle layer" next to RecurringTaskManager (the obligation layer) and HierarchyItemChecklistManager (the procedure layer). The three task tools together cover cadence, procedure and team execution.
  • Nonprofits and grant-funded organizations: funders ask "what did the team accomplish in this period?" - the sprint goal and the report view answer it. Grant-period sprints with exported PDFs become reporting artifacts.
  • Consultants and freelancers (B2B2B): run each client as an instance; the CMS instance scope already isolates client tasks, while shared sprints keep the consultant's own calendar.
Progress on the prerequisites: the engine is feature-complete and tested. What remains before a public launch: the rename to SprintTaskManager, a notification channel (due-date and sprint-close reminders via the CMS email SDK), and starter content packs per segment - the same pattern the RecurringTaskManager already uses with its DEFINITION-PACKS folder.

7Similar tools on the market

Yes - but nothing combines all four of its strengths (sprint goal as a first-class object, drag-and-drop kanban, built-in per-sprint reports, manager/staff permissions) in one lightweight package. Competitors cluster into four groups:

Group A - Kanban boards

Trello, MeisterTask, Kanbanize. Trello is the closest cousin: boards, cards, labels, attachments. Weaknesses for this use case: no native sprint/period object with a goal, no completion/overdue math, permissions are a paid feature, and a board drifts without a cycle boundary. MeisterTask adds automation but the same gaps apply.

Group B - Project management suites

Asana, Monday, ClickUp, Wrike, Notion (templates). All can do sprints, goals, views and reports - with configuration, training and usually a paid tier. Weaknesses: they are project-shaped (portfolios, dependencies, automations), so a two-week team cycle carries real setup and upkeep overhead, and reporting lives behind dashboard configuration.

Group C - Dev-focused agile tools

Jira, Linear, Shortcut (Clubhouse), Azure Boards. Purpose-built sprints, boards and reports. Weaknesses: they are software-shaped - story points, epics, releases - which makes them heavy and intimidating for non-technical teams; Linear and Jira also price per seat.

Group D - Vertical, workflow-locked tools

Tool / category What it does Overlap and gap vs this tool
Store and restaurant ops: Jolt, Zenput Per-location checklists and audits Same short-cycle execution idea, but locked to store workflows; no general team projects
Agency tools: Basecamp, Teamwork Client projects with messages and files Project-shaped and message-heavy; no sprint goal object, and higher per-seat pricing

8Positioning: the gap this tool fills

The gap

  • Kanban boards are shapeless - no period boundary, no goal, no completion math
  • Project suites are heavy - setup, training and price exceed what a 2-15 person team needs
  • Agile tools are developer-shaped and scare non-technical teams away
  • Spreadsheets carry the plan but no ownership, attachments or drag-and-drop

The position

"The sprint board for teams that do not do agile ceremony": time-boxed periods with a written goal, a drag-and-drop board, built-in completion and overdue reports, and role-based permissions - light enough for a volunteer team, disciplined enough for a manager who answers to a funder.

One-line pitches per audience: "every campaign closes with a report" (agencies), "show funders what the team did this period" (nonprofits), "a two-week plan on one board, printed if you want" (stores and crews).

9Why people will not use it - objections and fixes

Honest negative assessment. Every objection below is a real adoption blocker, ranked by how likely it is to stop someone from using the tool, with the fix that addresses it.

Severity Objection Why it blocks adoption What to do about it
High "No notifications or reminders" A due date that never taps a shoulder is a due date that misses. Teams default back to chat and email for nudges, and the tool becomes a passive wall. Ship due-date and sprint-close reminders through the CMS email SDK (already available). Later: a daily digest of overdue and due-today cards.
High "We already use Trello / Asana / Monday" Teams will not run two overlapping boards. A direct fight with entrenched suites is lost before it starts. Position as the light alternative, not the replacement: "your project suite plans the year, this tool runs the fortnight". Sell to teams currently on spreadsheets - they are the real target.
High "Everything must live in a sprint" Ad-hoc requests that arrive mid-period have no home; forcing a sprint for every task feels rigid and teams will work around the tool. Keep the sprint as the organizing frame but document the mid-sprint add flow prominently; the default two-week window is short enough that "add it to this sprint" is a natural answer.
Medium "No subtasks, dependencies or Gantt" Teams managing genuinely complex work (hardware, multi-party projects) will outgrow flat cards quickly. This is a positioning boundary, not a bug: point complex-project teams at a suite. Do not chase Gantt features; it would blur the tool's simplicity.
Medium "No recurring tasks" Teams with weekly obligations (reports, checks) will want them repeating inside the sprint. Deliberate split: RecurringTaskManager is the sibling for cadence. Link the two in messaging - "obligations recur, execution sprints".
Medium "No mobile app" Staff on the floor or in the field check tasks on phones; a desktop web page limits them. The layout is already responsive; a PWA wrapper with browser notifications closes most of the gap without a store submission.
Medium "Where did our last sprint go?" Teams want to compare periods or look up a past sprint's plan. Export the sprint to PDF/CSV at close (already possible) and store it with the funder/client file; later, a read-only sprint archive view.
Medium "No velocity or burndown" Teams trained on scrum will look for the classic charts and may dismiss the report view as light. The completion donut and workload bars answer "are we on track" for most teams; if needed, a burndown is one more SVG in the report view - low cost, do it only if a segment asks.
Low "Vendor lock-in - our plans are trapped" Data-portability fears stop careful buyers (finance, compliance, nonprofits). Already solved: PDF and CSV export per sprint. Document it as the official exit path.
Low "The name says Personal but it is a team tool" Confusion at first glance; buyers skip tools that look like solo toys. Solved by the rename to SprintTaskManager (section 2) plus a team-oriented landing description.

10Recommendation

  1. Rename to SprintTaskManager. Update the folder, file names (SprintTaskManager.html/.css/.js), the tool title and documentation. Keep the CMS type names (taskSprints-uniconbaseapps, taskItems-uniconbaseapps) unchanged - they are technical identifiers, not product names.
  2. Pick one beachhead segment. The strongest matches are nonprofits/charities (the current instance) and marketing/creative agencies - both already buy tools to run short pushes. The demo for either is the same: create a sprint, set the goal, drag three cards, open the report.
  3. Sell the cycle, not the board. The differentiator is the sprint object plus per-sprint reports - marketing should lead with "goal, deadline, ownership, proof", not "kanban".
  4. Ship the notification channel next. Due-date and sprint-close emails are the single highest-impact feature for retention and are already possible through the CMS email SDK.
  5. Treat the current instance as reference customer number one and produce a screenshot-and-numbers case study from it: sprints closed, tasks completed, overdue trend.
  6. Keep the trio story. SprintTaskManager (team execution in cycles), RecurringTaskManager (repeating obligations with evidence) and HierarchyItemChecklistManager (procedures and standards) are three distinct answers to three distinct questions - bundle them in messaging, not in code.
