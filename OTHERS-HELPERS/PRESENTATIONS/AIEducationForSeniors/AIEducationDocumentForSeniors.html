AI Education for Seniors

A friendly, practical course that turns artificial intelligence into an everyday helper. No technical background needed - just curiosity, a phone or computer, and about ten minutes of practice a day.

Audience: adults 60+ Level: beginner, hands-on Format: 6 sessions of 90 minutes Workshop: 2 sessions of about 50 minutes + Kahoot Tools: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, Perplexity + creator tools Language: English Version 1.6 - 26 Sep 2026

About this document

Everything needed to teach and learn AI as a useful life tool - one complete reference for organizers, instructors, helpers, and learners.

This document is the full course behind the slide decks. The complete 30-slide master deck is AIEducationPresentationForSeniors.html, and the two workshop decks - AIEducationPresentation-Session1.html (17 slides) and AIEducationPresentation-Session2.html (16 slides) - split it into two sessions of about 50 minutes each. This file is the internal source of truth for the program. The handout learners receive is AIEducationForSeniors-Handbook.html - a shorter, learner-only version with no instructor or technical details.

  • Learners can work through modules 1 to 10 at their own pace, alone or with a helper.
  • Instructors can follow the six-week session plans in the Instructor notes section and project the companion presentation.
  • Families can use the Practice sheets to support a parent or grandparent one exercise at a time.
The promise of this course After six sessions, every learner should be able to: open a chat with AI, ask a useful question, get a helpful answer in plain language, recognize when an answer may be wrong, and stay safe from AI-powered scams.

Built for older adults - and for the people who teach them.

  • Retirees who want to keep their minds active and stay independent.
  • Grandparents who want to stay close to grandchildren and family abroad.

  • One idea at a time. Each module teaches one skill and practices it before moving on.
  • Readable everything. Large text, high contrast, short paragraphs, printed handouts available.

A note on fears Many seniors worry that AI is "too complicated for me" or "something that will trick me." Both feelings are normal and both are addressed directly: the course shows how simple daily use is, and it teaches exactly how the tricks work so they can be spotted. Confidence grows fastest when a learner wins their first small success - so the very first exercise is designed to succeed.

AI in plain words

What it is, what it is not, and where it already lives in daily life - 20 minutes

The library-friend picture

The simplest picture that works: imagine a friend who has read every book, article, and recipe in the world - millions of pages - in every language. This friend cannot feel, does not know you personally, and sometimes remembers things wrong. But this friend is extremely fast at summarizing, explaining, writing, translating, and giving ideas. That friend is AI.

  • AI does not think like a person. It guesses the next word, again and again, based on everything it has read. The guesses are so good that the result looks like thinking.
  • AI does not know you. It only knows what you choose to type. It has no memory of your life unless you tell it (and some apps let you tell it, which can be useful).
  • AI is not always right. It can make mistakes with complete confidence. Important facts always need checking - this rule appears in every module.

AI is

A fast, patient, tireless assistant that never gets annoyed by questions.

AI is not

A person, a doctor, a lawyer, a bank, or an authority you should trust blindly.

AI needs

A clear question from you. The question is called a prompt.

AI is already in your daily life

  • The junk-mail filter that keeps spam out of your inbox.
  • The map app that predicts how long a drive will take.
  • The camera that sharpens your photos and finds a face in a crowd of old pictures.
  • The bank alert that spots an unusual purchase on your card.
  • The TV service that suggests what to watch next.
  • The voice assistant in your phone that sets a reminder when you say "remind me."

In short: AI has been helping inside your phone for years. Today you simply learn how to ask it for help - in your own words.

What comes next: the future of AI

Learners often ask where all this is going. The honest, useful answer: nobody can predict exactly, but two things are sure - AI will be in more places, and the five safety rules will stay exactly the same.

WhenWhat to expectWhat it means for you
Near future - 1 to 3 yearsAI built into every phone and computer. Better, more natural voice chats. Forms, letters, and translations finished in seconds with AI help. Doctors and banks use AI assistants - but a human always signs off.Fewer apps to learn: you will mostly talk to your phone. The asking skill you learn today stays the same.
Longer term - 5 to 15 yearsPersonal AI assistants that remember your appointments, medicines, and preferences. Health sensors at home watched over by your doctor. Smarter homes, self-driving transport, teaching that adapts to each person.AI removes busywork - it does not replace people. Decisions about money, health, and family stay human. One promise to make in class: the skill you learn today - asking well - never goes out of date. Whatever AI becomes, it will still answer the person who asks clearly.

Key words for this module

  • Artificial intelligence (AI): a computer program that imitates parts of human language and problem solving.
  • Chatbot: the chat window where you talk with AI by typing or speaking.
  • Prompt: the message or question you send. Everything starts with a prompt.
  • Reply: the answer the AI gives back.
Group warm-up Ask the class: "Where did a computer already help you this week without you asking?" Answers usually include maps, TV suggestions, spam filters, and bank alerts. That is AI already working for them.

Your first chat

Opening the app, signing in safely, and sending the first message - 45 minutes

Choose one tool to start

Pick exactly one. They all work the same way at the beginner level: a chat box at the bottom, answers above. The Tool guide compares them. For most seniors the easiest path is:

  • On a phone: install the ChatGPT app (free) from the app store, or simply use the voice assistant already built into the phone (Siri on iPhone, Google Assistant on Android).
  • On a computer: open any web browser and go to chatgpt.com, claude.ai, gemini.google.com, copilot.microsoft.com, or chat.deepseek.com. All have free plans - DeepSeek gives the most free daily use.

Signing in safely

  1. Create an account with your own email and a strong password you have not used elsewhere.
  2. Write the password in a paper notebook kept at home - do not save it in a shared computer.
  3. Never type your password, bank details, or ID numbers into the chat itself. The chat is for questions, not secrets.
  4. If the app asks for payment, look for the "free plan" option - all five tools have one. You never need to pay for this course.

Hands-on 1: the first three messages

Open the chat and type these, one at a time. Read each answer before sending the next.

Practice 1 - Say hello
Hello! My name is Mary and I am 72 years old. Please introduce yourself in three simple sentences and tell me three things you can help me with.

What to expect: a short, friendly answer that lists easy tasks like writing, explaining, and translating. This proves the chat works - the first small win.

Practice 2 - Ask the big question
What is artificial intelligence? Explain it like you would to a curious grandparent, in plain words, with one everyday example.

What to expect: a paragraph without jargon, using an example such as a helpful librarian or a recipe assistant.

Practice 3 - Get the menu
List 10 things I can ask you that would help me in my daily life. Keep the list simple and put the most useful ones first.

What to expect: a numbered list - letters, shopping lists, recipes, reminders, explanations, stories, translations. Learners circle the three they care about most; that list becomes their personal goals.

Chat manners that make everything easier

  • Write full sentences - the AI follows instructions, not keywords.
  • You can be polite, but you do not have to be - a clear order works too.
  • If the answer is too long: type "Make it shorter".
  • If the answer is confusing: type "Explain that again, simpler".
  • If the answer is wrong: type "That is not right, please check" - it will try again.
  • You never finish a chat "wrong." There is nothing to break. Typing more words is the only skill.

Projects: your own folders in the AI

Both ChatGPT and Claude offer Projects - folders where you keep several chats about one subject. One folder for health, one for family, one for the garden. The AI uses everything in the folder when it answers, so it already knows the background.

  • ChatGPT: in the side menu choose Projects, then New project. Give it a name and a short instruction, such as "I am 72. Explain simply. Answer in Turkish." Every chat inside the project follows that instruction.
  • Claude: in the side menu choose Projects, then create a project. Claude projects also take project knowledge - paste your favorite recipes, a medication list, or family first names once, and every chat in the project reads them.
  • Why use them: tidier chats, more consistent answers, and you never repeat yourself inside a project.
  • The same safety line applies: a project is still a chat - no passwords, no card numbers, no ID numbers inside it.

Memory: what the AI remembers about you

  • ChatGPT has a Memory. When you say "remember that I like short answers" - or it notices you always ask in Turkish - it saves that and uses it in later chats. See and manage it under Settings - Personalization - Memory, or simply ask: "What do you remember about me?"
  • Claude keeps less long-term memory. It remembers only inside the current chat. To make Claude remember things across chats, put them in a Project as project knowledge.
  • Get more customized answers: state your preferences once - "Always explain simply, answer me in Turkish, my name is Mary." Store it in ChatGPT memory or in the project instruction line. The AI then sounds like your own assistant.
  • Delete what it gathered: in ChatGPT say "Forget everything you remember about me", or delete items under Settings - Personalization - Memory. In Claude, delete the chat or the project files. Deleting a chat removes what is inside it in both apps.
  • The safe habit: let the AI remember only what you would say in a public library. Convenience must never beat the no-secrets rule.

Asking well

The one real skill of the whole course: the four-part question recipe - 45 minutes

AI answers exactly what it is asked. A vague question gets a vague answer. A rich question gets a useful answer. Asking well is the whole skill, and it has four parts.

The four-part recipe

PartQuestion to answerExample
1. WhoWho are you, and who is this for?"I am a 75-year-old grandmother..."
2. WhatWhat do you want, exactly?"...and I need a short birthday letter for my 10-year-old grandson..."
3. DetailsWhich details matter?"...he loves dinosaurs and football, and I want it warm but not long..."
4. FormatHow should the answer look?"...write about 100 words, in simple sentences."

Before and after

Weak prompt
write letter landlord

Result: a generic, impersonal letter that does not fit the real situation.

Strong prompt (same task, four parts)
I am a 74-year-old tenant. My bathroom tap has been dripping for two weeks and I have called the building manager twice with no result. Please write a short, polite letter to my landlord asking for a repair. Keep it under 150 words, friendly but firm, and leave placeholders for the date and my apartment number.

Result: a ready-to-use letter with the right tone, length, and details.

The five follow-up words that fix anything

  • "Shorter" - makes any answer more compact.
  • "Simpler" - removes jargon and long sentences.
  • "Bigger text" - in some apps you can ask for larger, clearer formatting in the answer itself.
  • "With an example" - turns an explanation into something concrete.
  • "Step by step" - turns a wall of text into numbered instructions.

The talk-and-improve loop: iterate with AI out loud

The best answers are not the first answers. AI drafts, you react, AI improves - and the whole loop can happen by talking, with no typing. Tap the microphone and speak your changes like you would to a patient assistant.

  1. Ask for the first draft. One clear four-part question.
  2. Read or listen. Ask: "Read it back to me" - the AI reads the draft aloud.
  3. Say one change. One small correction at a time: "make the opening warmer", "remove the second paragraph", "add my apartment number", "shorter".
  4. Hear the new version. The AI reads the improved draft back.
  5. Repeat until it sounds like you. Two or three rounds are normal; ten is fine too.
A worked example - the birthday letter, three rounds
Round 1: "Write a birthday letter for my grandson, about 80 words."

Round 2 (spoken): "Make the opening warmer, and mention that he is turning ten."

Round 3 (spoken): "Read it back to me. Then shorten it to 60 words and keep the joke about dinosaurs."

What to expect: each round is visibly better - and the whole loop happened by talking, with zero typing.

Iteration words worth teaching aloud

  • Small changes: "shorter", "simpler", "warmer", "more polite", "more formal", "bigger text".
  • Pointing at parts: "remove the second paragraph", "move the address to the end", "add one sentence about the refund".
  • Asking for options: "give me two versions - one formal, one warm", "what would make this better? three ideas".
  • Checking: "read it back to me", "check the dates", "translate it back into my language so I can check".
Try it - improve any document by talking
Take any letter you wrote this week. Open voice mode and say: "I will paste a letter. Read it back to me, then suggest three small improvements - and do only the ones I choose." Then say which ones to apply.

What to expect: the AI acts as a polite editor - you stay the author, and every change is yours to accept or refuse.

The same loop for ideas and big projects

  • Ideas: "give me five ideas for [topic]" - pick one - "develop idea 3 further" - "what could go wrong with it?"
  • Documents: draft - add - reorder - shorten - check facts - translate - read aloud - done. Each step is one spoken instruction.
  • Stories and books: one chapter at a time, one spoken correction at a time (see the family story project).

The live writing demo - a scripted path with a prompt palette

To show the class how a document becomes perfect by talking, print AIEducationForSeniors-LiveWritingDemo.html before the seminar. It holds the opening prompt ready to paste, plus a palette of universal follow-up prompts - so the next step is never a guess, even though nobody can know what the AI will write.

  1. Paste the opening prompt and show the first draft on the big screen.
  2. Ask: "Read it back to me." The room hears the draft.
  3. Look at the draft and pick ONE palette line that fits what you see - too cold? "Warmer." Too long? "Shorter."
  4. Say it and show the improved version.
  5. Repeat three rounds, then finish with "read it back to me" and a translate-back check.

Why this works: the palette lines are universal - they fix whatever appeared, so the next step is chosen by looking at the screen, never by guessing. And speaking every prompt instead of typing makes the loop look like magic.

Hands-on 2: fixing weak questions

Learners rewrite each weak prompt using the four parts, then compare answers with the strong version.

Practice 4 - Improve the question
Weak: "recipe chicken"

Strong version to try: I am cooking dinner for two on Saturday. Suggest a simple chicken recipe that takes under 45 minutes, uses common ingredients I likely have at home, and include step-by-step instructions.

What to expect: a full recipe with steps, instead of a vague suggestion.

Practice 5 - Your turn
Weak: "explain my blood test"

Learner task: rewrite this using all four parts (who, what, details, format). A strong version looks like: "I am 68. My doctor gave me blood test results and I do not understand the cholesterol numbers. Explain what LDL and HDL mean in plain words, what healthy ranges are, and list three questions I should ask my doctor. Do not diagnose anything."

Important habit AI can explain test results in plain words, but it must never decide what they mean for your health. Only a doctor or pharmacist can do that. Always ask AI for "questions to ask my doctor" - never for a diagnosis.

Everyday helpers

Letters, emails, forms, and planning - the work that fills an ordinary week - 50 minutes

This is where the course becomes visibly useful. Every learner has papers and messages they have been avoiding. AI does the drafting; the learner stays in charge and edits the result.

Letters and emails

  • Complaints: slow internet, wrong bill, faulty appliance.
  • Thanks: to a doctor, a neighbor, a volunteer, a teacher.
  • Invitations and replies: family gatherings, community events.
  • Requests: appointments, refunds, information, repairs.
Practice 6 - A polite complaint
Write a polite complaint letter to my internet company. My internet has been very slow for a week, I called three times, and nothing changed. I would like a refund for this month. My account number is [number]. Keep the letter short, respectful, and clear about what I want.

What to expect: a letter with a subject line, a calm explanation, and a clear request. Learner adds their own name and address and sends it.

Practice 7 - An appointment email
Draft a short, friendly email to my eye doctor asking for an appointment in the first week of October, in the afternoon if possible. I have been there before for an annual check.

What to expect: three to five polite sentences, ready to paste into the email app.

Understanding difficult letters

Banks, insurance companies, and government offices write long, complicated letters. AI can translate them into plain words in seconds.

Practice 8 - The plain-words explainer
Please explain this letter in plain words, like you are talking to a friend who is not a lawyer. Then tell me what I need to DO, step by step, and what deadlines I must not miss:

[paste the letter text here]

What to expect: a summary, an action list, and the deadlines pulled out - no jargon left.

Weekly planning

  • Menus and shopping lists for one person or two.
  • Packing lists for trips and hospital stays.
  • To-do lists sorted by what matters most.
  • Reminder schedules for bills, renewals, and appointments.
Practice 9 - The weekly helper
I have a busy week coming up: a doctor appointment on Tuesday, a pharmacy visit on Wednesday, and a family birthday dinner on Saturday. Help me plan my week day by day, with one reminder for each day, in a simple table.

What to expect: a day-by-day week plan with reminders - something to print and keep on the fridge.

The dictation shortcut

Typing is slow for many learners. Both phones and computers have a microphone: tap it and speak the prompt instead of typing it. The recipe stays the same - say "who, what, details, format" out loud. In the AI apps, tap the microphone next to the chat box and talk normally; the app writes it down and answers.

Health and wellbeing

A gentle companion for meals, food checks, medicines, and mind - with a clear safety line - 60 minutes

What AI can help with

  • Meal ideas inside dietary limits: low salt, low sugar, diabetic-friendly, soft food, smaller portions.
  • Gentle movement plans: seated exercise routines, short walks, stretching - always approved by the learner's doctor before starting.
  • Medication schedules: a clear table of what to take and when, built from the labels the learner photographs or types in.
  • Plain-word explanations of diagnoses, test terms, and medication leaflets - for understanding, never for decisions.
  • Mind and mood: conversation on a lonely afternoon, memory games, gratitude prompts, calming breathing routines.
The health safety line (repeat every session) AI gives ideas and explanations - it NEVER decides anything medical. No diagnosis, no dose changes, no stopping a medication, no emergency advice. Doctors, nurses, and pharmacists remain the only health authority. For symptoms, pain, or emergencies: call your doctor or local emergency number, not a chatbot.

Hands-on 3: three health prompts

Practice 10 - Meals within limits
I am 70 years old and my doctor asked me to eat less salt. Give me five simple low-salt dinner ideas, each with fewer than eight ingredients, that I can make without special kitchen tools. Do not give medical advice.

What to expect: five realistic dinners and why they fit a low-salt diet. Learner checks with their doctor or dietitian if unsure.

Practice 11 - A chair routine
Create a gentle 15-minute seated exercise routine for a beginner, using only a chair. List each exercise with simple instructions and how many seconds to do it. Add a note that I should ask my doctor before starting.

What to expect: five to seven seated movements with timings, plus the doctor note automatically.

Practice 12 - The medication table
I take four medicines at different times of day. Help me make a clear daily schedule table with columns for morning, noon, evening, and night. I will type in the medicines and doses from my labels. Keep the table big and easy to read.

What to expect: an empty or partially filled table the learner completes with their own medication labels - the AI builds the structure, the labels remain the source of truth.

Food: ingredients, allergies, and halal

Food questions come up every week. AI can read an ingredient list with you - but it cannot taste, cannot test, and cannot certify anything. The maker's label and certification marks stay the final word.

  • Ingredient explainer: paste any ingredient list and get each item explained in plain words, with its common effects.
  • Allergy check: name your allergens, paste the list, and AI flags possible problems - then the food maker confirms.
  • Halal check: AI explains which ingredients may come from animal sources or alcohol and what questions to ask - the official halal certification label remains the reliable confirmation.
  • Vegetarian, vegan, and other diets: name your diet - gluten-free, diabetic-friendly, low salt - and AI checks an ingredient list against it, in plain words. The maker and your doctor confirm.
  • Food effects: what is known about a food's effects versus what is myth - personal health effects are always checked with a doctor.
Practice 13 - The ingredient explainer
Please explain this food ingredient list in plain words. For each item, say what it is and any common effects it may have:

[paste the ingredient list from the package]

What to expect: a plain-word list, item by item - E-numbers, preservatives, and all.

Practice 14 - The allergy check
I am checking food for allergies. My allergens are [peanuts, milk, eggs...]. Please check this ingredient list and point out anything I should worry about, with a plain explanation for each:

[paste the ingredient list]

Remind me to confirm with the food maker and my doctor.

What to expect: flagged ingredients plus a reminder that the maker and doctor confirm - AI checks words, not the food itself.

Practice 15 - The halal check
Please help me check whether this food is halal. The food and ingredients: [describe the food or paste the ingredient list]. Explain which ingredients may come from animal sources or alcohol, what to ask the maker, and remind me that an official halal certification label is the most reliable check.

What to expect: a careful explanation of doubtful ingredients - with the certification label named as the authority.

Try it - the diet check
I follow this diet: [gluten-free / vegan / low sugar / ...]. Please check this ingredient list and tell me which items fit and which do not, with a plain explanation for each:

[paste the ingredient list]

What to expect: a clear fit-or-not list - with the maker's label and your doctor as the final confirmation.

The label is the authority AI reads lists - it cannot verify what is inside a package. For allergies, ask the maker directly. For halal, look for the certification mark. For personal health effects, ask your doctor.

Pharmacy: understanding medicines and checking health claims

Medicines come with long leaflets in small print. AI can translate them into plain words and prepare your questions - but only a pharmacist or doctor decides anything about doses.

  • Understand your medicine: what it is for, how it is usually taken, and common side effects - all in plain words from your label.
  • Questions for the pharmacist: AI prepares a clear, polite question list before you visit or call.
  • Health claim check: heard something on TV or WhatsApp? AI judges how likely it is to be true or false and points to official sources - the true-or-false workflow below.
Practice 16 - Understand your medicine
Please help me understand my medicine in plain words. From my label: [medicine name, dose, what the leaflet says]. Explain what it is usually for, how it is commonly taken, and common side effects. Then list five good questions to ask my pharmacist. Never change a dose - my pharmacist decides.

What to expect: a plain-word explanation plus a ready question list for the pharmacy visit.

Practice 17 - The health claim check
HEALTH CLAIM CHECK TASK. I heard this health claim: [the claim, e.g. "this drink cures arthritis in a week"]. Please say how likely it is to be true or false, what reliable official sources say about it, and how I can verify it. Point out warning signs like miracle cures and secret formulas. Never diagnose or prescribe.

What to expect: a true-or-false verdict with reasons, plus the official places to double-check.

The true-or-false workflow for health news

  1. Who says it? A named doctor or hospital, or "a friend of a friend"? Anonymous claims are the weakest.
  2. Where is the proof? Real health advice points to studies and official sources, not screenshots.
  3. Check an official source. MedlinePlus, WHO, or your country's health authority - search the claim there.
  4. Watch for red flags. "Miracle cure", "doctors hate this", "secret formula", "act now" - these words mean stop and verify.
  5. Ask a professional. Your doctor or pharmacist settles it in one visit.
Medicines: the only rule that matters AI explains labels - it never changes a dose, never stops a medicine, and never mixes medicines. Those decisions belong to your pharmacist and doctor alone.

Doctor visits made easier

A visit goes better with preparation. AI can help on all three sides of it: before, during, and after.

  • Before: make a plain-word list of what to tell the doctor, plus the questions to ask.
  • During: bring a printed medication list; for language help, open ChatGPT Voice (best) or Microsoft Copilot voice and hand the phone over.
  • After: tell AI what the doctor said in your own words - it rewrites it clearly and lists what to do next.
Practice 18 - The doctor visit helper

What to expect: a one-page visit plan - what to say, what to ask, and what to bring.

After the visit - summarize what you heard
I just came from the doctor. In my own words, this is what I remember: [describe it however you can, in any language]. Please rewrite it as a clear summary in plain words, list what I need to DO next, and note any parts I should double-check with the clinic.

What to expect: a clean summary to keep in your health folder - and a list of follow-up questions.

Companionship, used with care

Chatting with AI can ease loneliness, and many seniors enjoy memory games, story prompts, and daily conversation. Two gentle rules keep it healthy:

  • AI is a tool, not a replacement for people. Use it between visits - and use the confidence it builds to reach out to real friends.
  • If a chat starts to feel like a doctor, a therapist, or a best friend, take a break. For emotional struggles, a real person - family, clergy, a doctor, a helpline - is the right support.

AI for healthy aging: tools and benefits

Healthy aging is the whole picture - body, mind, safety, and connection. These tools work together as one daily routine, and none of them replaces your doctor.

  • Meals and movement plans - ChatGPT (best) or Claude: low-salt menus, seated exercise routines, and a gentle weekly plan, all within your doctor's limits. Benefit: independence in daily choices without guesswork.
  • Medication reminders - Medisafe, MyTherapy, or the phone's own reminder app: a clear daily pill schedule with alerts. Benefit: the right medicine at the right time - no forgotten doses.
  • Steps and activity - Apple Health (iPhone) or Google Fit (Android): the phone counts steps and walks by itself. Benefit: you see your own progress, and small wins keep you moving.
  • Mind and company - ChatGPT or Claude: daily memory games, one new fact each morning, and calm conversation. Benefit: a sharper, more active mind and company on quiet afternoons.
  • Brain games - Lumosity, Peak, or Elevate: a few minutes of games each day for memory, attention, and focus. Benefit: a daily mental workout that feels like play.
  • Hearing and vision - phone live captions and Be My Eyes: live captions turn spoken words into text on the screen as people talk, and the camera reads labels aloud. Benefit: full participation in conversations and daily life.
  • Living-alone check-in - Snug: one tap a day tells your family you are fine; a missed check-in alerts them. Benefit: peace of mind for you and your family.
  • Doctor visits - the doctor visit helper plus an AI voice translator: questions before, a plain-word summary after, translation during. Benefit: calmer visits where nothing important is forgotten.
The healthy-aging rule Use these tools daily for habits, and as preparation for professional care. Every plan, routine, and food idea still gets your doctor's approval before it becomes part of your life.

Safety first

The golden rules, the scams that use AI, and how to check anything - 60 minutes

The five golden rules

  1. Never share secrets in a chat. No passwords, no bank or card numbers, no ID numbers, no full addresses. The chat is for questions, not for secrets.
  2. Never send money because of a phone call or message. No real bank, police officer, or grandchild ever demands gift cards or urgent transfers. Hang up and call the real number yourself.
  3. AI does not call you first. No chatbot phones you about your account. If someone claims to be from Microsoft, Apple, your bank, or "AI support" - it is a scam call. Hang up.
  4. Verify before you trust. Important facts (health, money, travel documents) get checked against an official source - the doctor, the bank's own website, the government site.
  5. When in doubt, stop. Urgency is the scammer's tool. Real organizations give you time. If you feel rushed or scared, end the conversation and talk to family first.

  • What you type may be used to improve the AI. Treat the chat like a public library, not a diary.

  • If a chat holds something personal, delete the conversation when done (every major app has a delete option).
  • For very private matters, talk to a person - not a chatbot.

Independent practice

The ten-minute daily habit

Skills grow from small, regular use - not long sessions. The daily routine has three parts:

  1. One real task (5 minutes). Something actually needed: a shopping list, an email, a recipe, a question about the news.
  2. One follow-up (2 minutes). Use one of the five fix-up words: "shorter," "simpler," "with an example," "step by step," or "another version."
  3. One new thing (3 minutes). Ask a question about something new - a bird, a song, a place, a word. Curiosity keeps the mind sharp.

The weekly challenge list

Print and check off one per week:

  • Week 1: Use the dictation microphone instead of typing.
  • Week 2: Write one real letter or email with AI help and send it.
  • Week 3: Translate one message from or to another language.
  • Week 4: Interview AI about one of your hobbies.
  • Week 5: Make a packing list for a real or imaginary trip.
  • Week 6: Explain something to a grandchild using an AI explanation you prepared.
  • Week 7: Plan a full week of dinners plus one shopping list.
  • Week 8: Start your family story booklet - one interview.
  • Week 9: Use voice commands for all reminders for one full day.
  • Week 10: Teach one friend one thing you learned. Teaching is the proof.

Starting a practice club

  • Meet weekly for one hour at the library, community center, or a coffee shop with wifi.
  • Round one: each person shares the one thing they did with AI this week.
  • Round two: everyone tries the same new prompt together - "group practice."
  • Round three: one safety story from the news, discussed as a group.
  • More confident members become "table helpers" - teaching others is the fastest way to keep the skill.

Room setup and materials

  • Chairs with table space for phones, a large screen or projector for the slides, and strong wifi.

Teaching tips that work with seniors

  • Show, then do. Demonstrate once on the big screen, then let everyone try with a helper nearby.
  • Repeat the point three times. Say it, show it, then ask the class to say it back in their own words.
  • One success per session. End every session with each learner having completed at least one real task.
  • Never say "it is easy." For someone who fears technology, "easy" sounds like a judgment. Say "it takes a few tries" instead.
  • Patience over pace. If a session runs long on one exercise, that exercise was the valuable part - the curriculum can flex.
  • Normalize mistakes. Share one of your own mistakes with AI every session. Learners relax when the teacher errs too.
  • Phone-first. Most seniors will use AI on their phone. Where possible, teach on the phone with the computer as backup.

The friendly quiz (10 questions)

1. Is AI a person who secretly reads your messages?

No. AI is a computer program. It only sees what you type, and you should treat chats as public anyway.

2. What is a "prompt"?

The question or instruction you send to the AI. Everything starts with a prompt.

3. The answer is too long. What do you type?

"Make it shorter" - or any of the five fix-up words: shorter, simpler, step by step, with an example, another version.

4. Should you type your bank PIN into a chat?

Never. No passwords, card numbers, or ID numbers in any chat.

5. A caller sounds exactly like your grandson and asks for urgent money. What do you do?

Hang up and call his real number. Voices can be copied. Use the family code word.

6. Can AI make mistakes?

Yes - often, and with full confidence. Always verify important facts with official sources.

7. What is a deepfake?

A fake photo, video, or voice made with AI, designed to look real.

8. Name one daily task AI can help with.

Letters and emails, shopping lists, menus, reminders, translation, explanations, stories - any one is correct.

9. Who should decide medical questions?

Doctors, nurses, and pharmacists. AI gives ideas and explanations, never decisions.

10. How much daily practice keeps the skill alive?

Ten minutes a day - one real task, one fix-up, one curious question.
