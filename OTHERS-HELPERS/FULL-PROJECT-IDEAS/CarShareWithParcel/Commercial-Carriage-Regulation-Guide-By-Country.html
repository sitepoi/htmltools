1The Two Models and the Core Legal Truth

Model A - Passenger carpool (strict cost-sharing)

  • Driver receives only a share of actual trip costs (fuel, tolls, wear). No profit, no pricing freedom beyond the cost-sharing formula.
  • This model has explicit legal recognition in almost every country reviewed here - it is treated as private, non-commercial travel and needs no transport licence.
  • The single rule that keeps it legal: the payment must never look like profit. Most jurisdictions cap or define what may be shared.

Model B - Cargo cost-sharing (a parcel contributes instead of a passenger)

  • The sender pays a contribution toward trip costs (sized like a seat contribution), the parcel rides in the trunk, and the platform takes a small service fee.
  • Pricing and messaging mirror the carpool model: "your parcel shares the trip cost", not "we sell courier shipping".

The core legal truth - read this before anything else

Passenger carpooling has a cost-sharing exemption almost everywhere. Cargo does not. In most jurisdictions any payment to carry another person's goods makes the trip "for hire" - the law does not care whether the fee only covers fuel. So "cost-sharing with cargo" is a business and pricing strategy, not a legal category. What actually decides cargo legality is different everywhere: (a) vehicle weight thresholds (UK: no licence under 3.5 t, Belgium: under 500 kg payload), (b) deregulated freight (Ontario, Alberta, BC), (c) broker/carrier authority (USA), and (d) enforcement culture. This guide is the map of those four factors.

3USA - Federal Framework

Model A - passenger carpool

  • Legal in all 50 states and DC when compensation is only cost-sharing. Many state statutes codify a carpool or "ridesharing arrangement" exemption inside the TNC law (Virginia's is the clearest).
  • Interstate carpool trips are treated as private travel - no federal permit applies as long as no profit is made.
  • The trap: if the payment exceeds a genuine share of costs, the trip becomes a commercial TNC ride and state TNC permits, background checks and TNC insurance all kick in. Pricing must be a capped cost-sharing formula.

Model B - cargo contribution (no exemption exists)

  • Interstate: carrying property for any compensation is for-hire transportation under FMCSA rules. There is no cost-sharing exemption for cargo. The compliant structure is the broker model: the platform holds FMCSA broker authority (MC number + USD 75,000 surety bond + BOC-3 process agent + UCR) and arranges transport through properly insured drivers. Roadie (UPS-owned) and similar platforms run this way.
  • Intrastate (inside one state): rules vary - some states deregulated general freight, most require intrastate for-hire registration, and household-goods moves are regulated nearly everywhere. See the state table.
  • Insurance reality: personal auto policies almost always exclude carrying goods for compensation. The platform must carry a cargo policy per trip (or blanket), plus commercial coverage on the driver side - never rely on personal policies.

Practical reading for the USA

Model A is easy everywhere (cost-sharing only). Model B is doable but must run on the broker-authority structure - the "cost-sharing contribution" wording does not exempt it, it only keeps the pricing consumer-friendly. Start cargo in states with light freight regulation (FL, TX, AZ, GA).

5Canada - Federal / Provincial Framework

Model A - passenger carpool

  • Legal in all 10 provinces when compensation is only a share of actual expenses. Quebec has the clearest statutory "covoiturage" exemption (the model Amigo Express runs on); BC's Passenger Transportation Board explicitly excludes non-commercial carpools; Ontario permits cost-sharing arrangements without a licence.
  • Profit triggers the TNC/taxi regime, so pricing must stay a capped cost-sharing formula. For-profit passenger transport is out of scope for this project.
  • Interprovincial cost-sharing carpool is treated as private travel - the safe design for cross-border corridors.

Model B - cargo contribution

  • Intra-provincial: most provinces deregulated freight economics decades ago - Ontario, Alberta, BC, Saskatchewan and Manitoba require no operating authority for intra-provincial truck freight, only safety registration (National Safety Code / CVOR-style, applying to commercial vehicles) and commercial insurance. A private car carrying parcels needs correct courier insurance and business registration - personal policies will not cover it.
  • Quebec is the exception: the Commission des transports du Quebec (CTQ) permits for-hire goods carriers and brokers - cargo sharing in Quebec needs CTQ structure, making it the hardest cargo province.
  • Extra-provincial (a paid parcel crossing a provincial border): federally governed - extra-provincial undertakings need a safety fitness certificate and, for trucks, provincially issued extra-provincial authority. A private car carrying a paid parcel across a border sits in a grey zone; start intra-provincial and structure cross-border lanes through licensed carriers.

Practical reading for Canada

Canada is one of the friendliest places on earth for both models: carpool cost-sharing is legal in all 10 provinces, and light cargo inside most provinces is deregulated. Friction points: Quebec's CTQ for cargo, and the extra-provincial layer for any paid cross-border parcel.

7Europe - Country Review

United Kingdom

Model A: legal when only expenses are shared. Model B: a goods vehicle operator licence ("O-licence") is required only above 3.5 t gross weight - a car or small van doing courier work needs no operator licence, just business registration and "hire and reward" insurance. One of the easiest countries for both models. Note: Uber's UK Supreme Court ruling makes drivers "workers" - design the driver relationship carefully.

EASY Employment-law note

France

Model A: covoiturage is legal and state-promoted, but strictly cost-sharing - the 2015 Thevenoud law banned for-profit carpooling. Model B: strict - even light commercial vehicles under 3.5 t doing public goods transport must register in the national transport register and prove "light transport professional capacity" (certified manager). A private car carrying parcels for a contribution has no legal frame here.

DIFFICULT for cargo Carpool EASY

Germany

Model A: Mitfahrzentralen (cost-sharing platforms) legal; drivers may only share actual costs. Model B: freight licence (GüKG) needed above 3.5 t; under 3.5 t no freight licence, but the courier business must be registered (Gewerbe) with courier insurance. Light cargo cost-sharing is workable.

EASY - MODERATE

Spain

Model A: legal cost-sharing (Blablacar.es). Model B: public goods transport requires a transport authorization (tarjeta), extended in recent reforms down to light vehicles (MDL category) - heavy compliance for a P2P parcel model.

DIFFICULT for cargo

Italy

Model A: legal cost-sharing. Model B: for-hire goods carriers register with the national road transport register (Albo autotrasportatori) depending on vehicle class; light courier work possible with registration and insurance.

MODERATE

Netherlands

Model A: legal cost-sharing. Model B: no special transport licence for light vehicles - business registration and professional insurance suffice. One of the easiest EU cargo jurisdictions.

EASY Drivers ruled employees in a 2021 court case

Belgium

Model A: legal covoiturage. Model B: transport for third parties needs a licence above 500 kg payload; under 500 kg payload is exempt - small parcels in a private car are licence-free in practice.

EASY - MODERATE

Nordics (Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland)

Model A: legal cost-sharing (Denmark is strict if profit appears - Uber left in 2017). Model B: goods permits (godskørselstilladelse / løyve / trafiktillstånd) required above 3.5 t; light vehicles generally exempt. Nimber (Norway) ran light crowdshipping until it shut down for business reasons in 2020, not legality.

EASY - MODERATE

Poland

Model A: legal cost-sharing. Model B: domestic road transport licence required above 2.5-3.5 t; light vehicles below the threshold do domestic courier work licence-free. Accessible market for a light cargo MVP.

EASY - MODERATE

EU overlay

Since the 2017 European Court of Justice ruling, platforms matching paid transport are treated as transport services in each member state - national law applies directly. Cost-sharing carpool platforms sit in a lighter frame, but cargo always lands in the national goods-transport regime.

8Asia-Pacific - Country Review

Japan

Model A: carrying people for payment requires commercial vehicle registration (green plates) and a Class 2 driver licence; private-car ridesharing is illegal outside limited taxi-supervised pilots. Model B: commercial goods transport also requires business-use registration - private "white plate" cars may not carry for hire.

NO-GO for both models

South Korea

Model A: paid transport in private cars is illegal; carpool apps tolerated only for commuting-style use. Model B: private cars carrying goods for pay restricted to licensed transport businesses.

NO-GO

Singapore

Model A: non-commercial carpooling (GrabHitch model) allowed but capped at 2 trips per day per driver. Model B: private cars must be converted to goods vehicles for courier use - possible but regulated.

MODERATE

India

Model A: carpool apps (BlaBlaCar India, QuickRide) run on cost-sharing and are accepted. Model B: light courier is easy - GST registration, insurance and local permits; no heavy licensing for small vehicles.

EASY - MODERATE

Australia

Model A: casual carpooling cost-sharing permitted in every state (ride-hail is separately accredited; drivers must register for GST regardless of income). Model B: light couriers need an ABN, insurance and vehicle standards - no heavy licensing.

EASY

New Zealand

Model A: carpool cost-sharing exempt from small passenger service licensing. Model B: a goods service licence is required to carry goods for hire - a real but obtainable step.

MODERATE

9Americas, Middle East and Turkey

Brazil

Model A: BlaBlaCar Brazil runs intercity carpooling on cost-sharing, broadly accepted. Model B: freight is ANTT-regulated for carriers, but light courier businesses operate with standard registration and insurance.

EASY - MODERATE

Mexico

Model A: carpooling cost-sharing generally tolerated; ride-hail is state-regulated and some states ban it. Model B: for-hire freight requires a federal permit (SCT); small parcels in private cars is a grey zone with low enforcement.

MODERATE State-by-state variance

United Arab Emirates (Dubai / Abu Dhabi)

Model A: private carpooling for payment is not permitted - only RTA-licensed e-hail operators. Model B: courier work requires a trade licence and RTA/municipal permits. Everything is licensed, nothing is casual.

DIFFICULT

Saudi Arabia

Model A: private carpooling for money not permitted; ride-hail only via licensed operators. Model B: licensed courier businesses only.

DIFFICULT

Turkey

Model A: intercity carpooling (BlaBlaCar Türkiye is widely used) sits in a tolerated grey zone with periodic legal pressure; commercial passenger transport requires a D2 authorization. Model B: commercial goods carriage requires K-type authorizations (K1/K3); a private car carrying parcels for strangers for a contribution is a grey zone. Cheap intercity bus cargo is the incumbent substitute.

MODERATE - DIFFICULT Court rulings shift the carpool status - re-verify before launch

12Launch Compliance Checklist

  1. Price as cost-sharing, not fares: passenger contributions capped to a documented cost formula (km-based fuel + tolls share); cargo contributions sized as a seat-equivalent share. Publish the formula - it is the best evidence of non-commercial intent in every carpool jurisdiction.
  2. Cargo legal structure per market: FMCSA broker authority (USD 75,000 bond) in the USA; CTQ permits in Quebec; respect vehicle weight thresholds in the EU/UK (3.5 t, 500 kg Belgium, 2.5 t Poland) so drivers stay under the licence line; use licensed carriers for cross-border lanes in Canada.
  3. Insurance stack: platform cargo policy per trip (or blanket policy), verified parcel add-on, courier / hire-and-reward coverage on the driver side. Personal auto policies are invalid for this activity - never rely on them.
  4. Driver onboarding: ID and background checks, licence validation, vehicle inspection, written confirmation that the driver only shares costs (passengers) and acts under the platform's authority (cargo).
  5. Payment design: escrow, no cash payouts before delivery, cost-sharing caps enforced in the app so a driver cannot turn a shared trip into a commercial one.
  6. Taxes: platform collects/remits where required (GST/HST for Canadian rideshare drivers, GST for Australian drivers, VAT treatment in the EU).
  7. Employment-law review: UK, Netherlands and New Zealand treat drivers as workers/employees - design the driver relationship with local counsel.
  8. Monitor legal drift: Turkey's carpool status, Korea's carpool rules, and US state rules change frequently - re-verify quarterly.
