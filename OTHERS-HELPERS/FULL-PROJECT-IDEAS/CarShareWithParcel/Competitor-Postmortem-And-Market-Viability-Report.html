2Postmortems - The Dead and the Diminished

Deliv (USA, 2012-2020) - the best-funded casualty

Same-day crowdshipping for mall operators and retailers. Raised US$12.35M from the four largest US mall operators plus General Catalyst, Redpoint and strategic money from UPS, Google, Enterprise and GM. Expanded to 33 markets and 1,400 cities by 2017. Shut down its service in 2019; Target bought the technology assets in May 2020 and folded the team into Target.

Good points: enterprise partnership model (malls, retailers), strong brand of backers, real same-day demand, nationwide expansion playbook.
Failure points: last-mile unit economics against Amazon Prime Now and Walmart's own networks; gig supply and support costs per order; no consumer brand of its own - it died as a middleman between retailers and shoppers.

Shyp (USA, 2013-2018) - US$62M and still died

On-demand pickup, packaging and shipping app. Raised US$62.1M led by Kleiner Perkins. In 2015 it converted couriers from independent contractors to W-2 employees. Closed New York, LA and Chicago in 2017; dissolved entirely in March 2018.

Good points: genuinely loved convenience product, shipping price optimization, premium experience.
Failure points: the pickup courier cost more than the revenue per shipment; W-2 employees multiplied that cost; one-off usage with no repeats; burned through all funding without reaching contribution margin.

Nimber (Norway, 2013-2020) - the closest model to ours, and it died

Pure peer-to-peer intercity crowdshipping: people going between Norwegian cities carried parcels for others. Wound down in March 2020. This is the most important case in this whole report because it is the exact model we are proposing.

Good points: genuine "already traveling" supply, low-cost operation, real users in a cost-sharing-friendly culture.
Failure points: a small country (Norway) never reached corridor liquidity; tiny per-parcel contributions could not fund support and trust operations; it ran out of time before density arrived - and the 2020 pandemic finished it. The model was not disproven; it was starved.

PiggyBee (Belgium, 2012 - closed) - the founder's own postmortem

Travelers carried parcels for others, mostly internationally, with pricing negotiated per trip. After 12+ years and roughly 30,000 users, the founder closed the platform: "the income still does not cover the expenses". His farewell message is the most valuable primary source in this entire report - he names exactly what killed it.

Good points: 30,000 users over 12 years proves the concept attracts people; low burn; an honest founder who published the lessons instead of hiding them.
Failure points (his words): (1) package inertia - "a package does not move on its own"; the first mile (parcel to the traveler) and last mile (traveler to the receiver) needed a human at every step, and the reward never covered the hassle; (2) no recurrence - "nobody wants Cuban cigars 20 times a year", so retention stayed fatally low; (3) airline reluctance to carry third-party parcels; (4) the resources required never paid back. His best user (a flight attendant) solved it by having parcels sent to his hotel and shipping back via relay points - a pattern he says is part of the future solution.

The early 2010s wave - Stuff2Send, Zipments, mmMule, Friendshippr

The first crowdshipping generation: match travelers or neighbors with parcels (Stuff2Send 2009, Zipments 2011, mmMule 2012, Friendshippr 2013). All faded or closed without ever reaching meaningful volume.

Good points: proved the concept was obvious to many founders at once; the US Postal Service itself studied the category in 2014.
Failure points: none had the trust stack (verification, escrow, insurance, tracking) that makes strangers comfortable; social-graph trust (Friendshippr) does not create volume; no pricing engine; no insurance partner.

Grabr (USA, 2016-) - travel-dependent, customs-grey

Travelers buy items abroad and deliver them in person to buyers at home. Alive but small; its supply of travelers collapsed during the 2020 travel shutdown. VERIFY current status.

Good points: a clear, urgent use case (imports without international shipping), buyer-traveler incentive alignment.
Failure points: sits in a customs grey zone (travelers importing goods for resale value), supply exists only when people fly, and each transaction needs personal negotiation.

3Postmortems - The Survivors

Roadie (USA, 2014-) - the one big win, acquired by UPS 2021

Gig delivery matching drivers with deliveries "on their way". US$10M Series A (2015), US$15M B (2016), US$37M C (2019, Home Depot participated). Anchors: Delta Air Lines luggage (2015), Home Depot (2017), Walmart grocery and general merchandise (2019-2020), Tractor Supply (2020). UPS acquired it in September 2021; at that point it reported 200,000+ drivers across 20,000+ ZIP codes.

Good points: enterprise anchors created driver density everywhere at once (national, not city-by-city); neutral meetup points (the Waffle House partnership); API-first B2B revenue; celebrity marketing (Ludacris, Jimmy Kimmel) for driver supply.
Weak points (from our angle): in practice Roadie drivers take dedicated gigs - the "on-the-way" framing is marketing; its business became retail last-mile logistics for UPS, and small consumer-to-consumer intercity parcels are not its core. It does not compete with our corridor model.

uShip (USA, 2003-) - the niche auction house

Reverse-auction marketplace for vehicles, boats, LTL freight and household moves. Benchmark Capital funding in 2005, the "Shipping Wars" TV series 2012-2015, eBay Motors integration. Still operating after 20+ years.

Good points: deep niche (odd-size items couriers refuse), auction pricing discovers real prices, massive free TV marketing.
Weak points: low-frequency, high-ticket items; participants are semi-professional carriers with authority - not occasional travelers; nothing for daily small parcels.

CitizenShipper (USA, 2008-) - the vertical specialist

Auction marketplace specialized in pets, vehicles and furniture transport, with escrow via Escrow.com integrated from 2013. Still operating.

Good points: specialization (pet transport is emotional and unserved), escrow from day one built trust, long-tail categories big couriers ignore.
Weak points: auctions favor experienced shippers; low frequency and high acquisition cost per order; not a daily parcel flow.

Zoom2u (Australia, 2014-) - the courier marketplace

Australian marketplace matching senders with car and van couriers, with a B2B API; listed on the ASX in 2021. Still operating. VERIFY current status.

Good points: real courier demand (Australia Post gaps), recurring business users, white-label API.
Weak points: drivers do dedicated jobs - it is an on-demand courier network, not trunk sharing; economics are courier economics.

Tiptapp (Sweden, 2016-) - fixed-price simplicity

P2P app for moving and collecting things at fixed prices, active in Sweden and the UK.

Good points: dead-simple fixed pricing (no negotiation, no auction), reuse and waste angle, low operational complexity.
Weak points: small tickets, mostly urban; does not target the intercity lane.

Trunkrs (Netherlands, 2015-) and Tıkla Gelsin (Turkey, ~2015-)

Trunkrs runs same-day crowdshipping for e-commerce in the Netherlands; Tıkla Gelsin runs P2P same-city delivery in Turkey. Both still operating. VERIFY Trunkrs current model.

Good points: e-commerce integration (Trunkrs) and local demand validation (Tıkla Gelsin) prove the P2P delivery category has real, paying demand.
Weak points: both are city-level or retailer-anchored; neither runs the intercity already-traveling trunk model.

4The Passenger-Side Context

  • BlaBlaCar (France, 2006): the proof that cost-sharing intercity travel works at scale - 100M+ members across Europe, India, Brazil, Turkey. Crucially, it never built a mainstream cargo lane - which is our window, and also the warning that it could.
  • Waze Carpool (USA, 2018-2022): Google's commuter carpool product shut down in 2022 - even a tech giant could not make US carpooling work without a payments/monetization engine. Lesson: the corridor and the pricing model matter more than the brand.
  • Poparide (Canada, 2015-): regional intercity carpool in BC/Alberta/Ontario/Quebec corridors - still operating, proving Canadian intercity demand, passengers only.
  • Amigo Express (Quebec): the Quebec covoiturage network under the province's explicit cost-sharing exemption - the legal blueprint we follow for the passenger lane.

1. No liquidity - supply and demand never met in the same corridor at the same timeNimber, PiggyBee, mmMule, FriendshipprCorridor-first launch, one dense lane at a time, driver supply recruited from real commuter communities before demand opens
2. No trust stack - no escrow, no verification, no insuranceEarly 2010s wave (Stuff2Send, Zipments, Friendshippr)Mandatory from day one per our operations manual: ID verification, escrow, delivery codes, insurance add-on

4. Unit economics beaten by subsidized giantsDeliv, ShypNo couriers, no packaging, no W-2 employees - the platform only moves money and matches trips; contribution margin per order is the only metric that matters

6The Founder's Own Words - Do His Difficulties Apply to Us?

PiggyBee's founder closed with a public postmortem. His diagnosis is the best map we have of what will go wrong - so each point is checked against our model below.

His two core observations

"The biggest problem with the model is... the inertia of the package" - a package does not move on its own; someone must handle every step. And "the lack of recurrence... the Achilles heel" - everyone needs a place to stay, a way to move, food; nobody needs the last Yankees t-shirt 20 times a year.
His difficultyApplies to us?Our answer
Package inertia - first mile and last mile at both endsYes, but structurally much smallerPiggyBee's parcels had to reach a hotel and leave an airport - detours with no shared route. Our driver's trip starts in the sender's city and ends in the receiver's city: both miles collapse into the route itself. What remains is handover choreography - on-route handover points, QR codes and a hard detour budget from the operations manual.
The reward never covered the hassleYes - the permanent design constraintFixed cost-sharing pricing, pre-agreed windows and zero negotiation remove the hassle PiggyBee had; the contribution only has to cover a 10-15 minute handover on the driver's way, not airport logistics.
No recurrence - one-off exotic itemsYes - the deepest shared riskHis demand was rare international one-offs. Ours is built on repeat senders - marketplace sellers, small businesses, students, urgent documents - with retention targets above 30%. If C2C one-offs dominate, we catch his disease; the corridor plan exists to avoid it.
Relay points are the carriers' secret (his Olivier example, Hytchers' petrol stations)A design hint, not a difficultyWe can borrow it: partner locations (campuses, universities, shops) become on-route relay points. Hytchers' fate - overtaken by traditional carriers and unions - is also a warning to prepare for incumbent pushback.
One-click ease of use (Vinted-style label printing)Yes - an adoption requirementSOP-1 already requires one-tap listing; we add one-click parcel label and QR printing at listing and at delivery - exactly the pattern he names.
International, airports, airlines, customsNo - avoided by designDomestic lanes only, cars only, no airports, no customs, no airline partners. PiggyBee fought the hardest version of this problem; we deliberately fight the easiest.
Cocolis lesson: territory focus + a clear niche ("what problem are you solving")Confirms our strategyHis closing advice is literally our plan: one territory (a province), a known pattern (carpool cost-sharing), a clear niche (same-day urgent and fragile light cargo under 60 kg).

The verdict on his question

He writes that he knows of no service that fully succeeded at P2P parcel delivery. That is consistent with this report - nobody has. But his own postmortem points at the viable variant: domestic, territory-focused, relay-point thinking, one-click ease. That variant is exactly what the corridor plan in this folder is. We inherit his two hard problems - package inertia and recurrence - reduced in size, not eliminated.

7Six Success Factors - Why the Survivors Lived

  1. Enterprise anchor volume: Roadie's retailer and airline contracts created driver density everywhere - our equivalent is university and business-corridor partnerships in the launch province.
  2. Deep vertical niche: uShip (freight), CitizenShipper (pets) - our niche is "same-day intercity small cargo by cars already on the road", a lane nobody owns.
  3. Trust from day one: CitizenShipper wired escrow in from the start - non-negotiable for us too.
  4. Fixed, simple pricing: Tiptapp's fixed prices beat auctions for ordinary users - our cost-sharing formula is this lesson applied to intercity lanes.
  5. Small, cash-light, one-country focus: Zoom2u, Tiptapp and Tıkla Gelsin survived without billions because they stayed lean and regional - our Wave 1 plan (one corridor in one province) is deliberately the same shape.
  6. Being acquired as infrastructure: Roadie (UPS) and Deliv's tech (Target) - the realistic premium exit is becoming the intercity trunk network of a big courier; worth designing the platform so a UPS/DPD-style buyer could plug it in.

The honest competitive truth

Every region has couriers, delivery apps and carpool platforms - but no one anywhere runs the combination we propose: intercity, already-traveling cars, cost-sharing contributions, fixed pricing, small parcels. Nimber was the only pure attempt, in a market (Norway) too small to feed it. The lane is empty; the question is whether corridor liquidity can be built before the incumbent copycats arrive.

9The Gap Our Model Targets

  • Not courier economics: we never pay drivers for dedicated trips - that is what killed Shyp and constrained Roadie. The driver's trip exists first; the parcel joins it.
  • Not negotiation: fixed cost-sharing contributions remove the friction that starved PiggyBee.
  • Not a small market: Nimber died in Norway (~5M people); our launch corridors in Ontario, BC and Alberta are bigger, and the playbook replicates province by province.
  • Not trust-lite: escrow, verification, codes and insurance from day one - the exact stack the early 2010s wave never built.
  • Not retail last-mile: Deliv died fighting Amazon for same-day mall delivery; we avoid that war entirely and serve a segment couriers price out.

10Market Viability Verdict

Viable as a lean, corridor-first regional business - not as a venture-scale bet

The evidence supports a narrow conclusion: the category has real, paying demand (Roadie, Tiptapp, Tıkla Gelsin, CitizenShipper prove it), but every pure P2P intercity attempt failed on liquidity and unit economics, and the only scale win (Roadie) survived by becoming a corporate courier network. Our model is designed around exactly those two failure modes - corridor liquidity first, zero courier costs - so it is viable as a focused regional operation with these conditions:

  • Launch corridor by corridor, never nationally (Nimber's mistake was a small market; Deliv's was too many markets).
  • Fixed cost-sharing pricing and full trust stack from day one (PiggyBee's and the early wave's mistakes).
  • No dedicated-trip payouts ever (Shyp's mistake).
  • Repeat senders before marketing spend (the one metric every survivor shares).
  • Expect a regional outcome: sustainable cash flow or an acquisition as trunk-network infrastructure - not a venture-scale exit.
