1The Free-Market Filter

The rule that decides what appears in this document: cargo regulation difficulty 1 or 2 on the companion guide scale - meaning light vehicles carry for hire with no transport licence, only business registration and proper insurance. Anything that requires an operating authority, a federal permit, a carrier registration with a professional exam, or sits in a legal grey zone is filtered out.

  • FREE - difficulty 1 No licence at all for light vehicles: UK, Netherlands, Belgium.
  • FREE - difficulty 2 Registration + insurance only, no transport licence: Germany, Australia, Florida, Ontario, British Columbia, Alberta, Poland, India, Nordics.
  • FILTERED OUT - difficulty 3 to 5 Broker authority, federal permits, carrier registration, capacity exams or grey zones required: Texas, California, Turkey, France, Spain, Brazil, Mexico, Quebec, New Zealand - plus the NO-GO countries (Japan, South Korea, UAE, Saudi Arabia). Listed with reasons in section 8.

3Market Reports - One Page Each

1United Kingdom - SAM 7.4 M EUR

68 M x 4 parcels x 15% fit x 6.5 EUR = 245 M TAM; x 25% corridors x 12% niche = 7.4 M SAM. No goods licence under 3.5 t, courier insurance widely available, London-Manchester lanes are dense. The cleanest big cargo market in Europe - launch London-Birmingham and London-Manchester.

  • Watch: driver worker-status law; hire-and-reward insurance required per driver.

2Germany - SAM 9.1 M EUR

84 M x 5 parcels x 15% fit x 6 EUR = 378 M TAM - Europe's largest parcel market. Light vehicles need no freight licence, only Gewerbe registration and courier insurance. Berlin-Hamburg and Frankfurt-Munich lanes carry heavy business travel.

  • Watch: strict cost-sharing rules if the contribution looks like profit; registration per courier business.

3Australia - SAM 3.8 M EUR

27 M x 3.5 parcels x 15% fit x 9 EUR (long 660-920 km lanes justify higher contributions) = 128 M TAM. ABN + insurance, no heavy licensing. Sydney-Melbourne is the single best long lane in the ranking.

  • Watch: drivers must register for GST regardless of income (ATO rule).

4Netherlands - SAM 2.6 M EUR

18 M x 4 parcels x 15% fit x 5 EUR = 54 M TAM, with a 40% corridor share because the Randstad is the corridor. No transport licence for light vehicles. Small but frictionless - a perfect testing ground for the EU playbook.

  • Watch: drivers ruled employees in a 2021 court case - design the driver relationship.

5Florida - SAM 3.2 M EUR

23 M x 4 parcels x 15% fit x 6.5 EUR = 90 M TAM; Miami-Orlando-Tampa lanes with light freight regulation and statewide preemption. The easiest US cargo state by compliance burden.

  • Watch: tourism seasonality - build the resident business base.

6Ontario - SAM 2.8 M EUR

16 M x 4 parcels x 15% fit x 7 EUR = 67 M TAM with a 35% corridor share - Toronto's lanes are the densest in Canada. Intra-provincial freight is deregulated: safety registration and courier insurance only.

  • Watch: stay intra-provincial at launch - cross-province parcels trigger the federal layer.

7Belgium - SAM 1.5 M EUR

12 M x 4 parcels x 15% fit x 5 EUR = 36 M TAM; 35% corridor share around Brussels-Antwerp-Liege. Exempt under 500 kg payload - small parcels in a private car are licence-free. Small but zero friction.

  • Watch: cross-border lanes to NL/FR change the rules - stay domestic first.

8Poland - SAM 1.7 M EUR

37 M x 3.5 parcels x 15% fit x 4.5 EUR = 87 M TAM; Warsaw-Krakow and Warsaw-Gdansk lanes. Licence-free under 2.5 t for domestic courier work. Large intercity market with rising e-commerce.

  • Watch: licence needed above 2.5 t - enforce the vehicle limit in onboarding.

9India - SAM 1.4 M EUR

250 M urban corridor population x 1.2 parcels x 20% fit x 3 EUR = 180 M TAM; small EUR tickets shrink the SAM, but the volume is enormous - a volume play, not a value play. GST + insurance, no heavy licensing for light couriers.

  • Watch: state-level licences vary - pick one state first; scale by state, not nationally.

10Nordics - SAM 1.4 M EUR

27 M x 3 parcels x 15% fit x 6.5 EUR = 79 M TAM across Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland. Light vehicles exempt from goods permits; mature cost-sharing culture. Fragmented - treat as one market only for reporting.

  • Watch: Denmark is strict on the passenger side - cargo lane only.

11British Columbia - SAM 0.9 M EUR

5.6 M x 4 parcels x 15% fit x 7 EUR = 24 M TAM; x 30% corridors x 12% niche = 0.9 M SAM. Intra-provincial freight is deregulated: National Safety Code registration for commercial vehicles, courier insurance for private cars. Vancouver-Kelowna and Vancouver-Kamloops are the lanes.

  • Watch: Vancouver-Calgary crosses into Alberta - the extra-provincial layer applies; skip Vancouver Island lanes at launch (ferry cost eats the contribution).

12Alberta - SAM 0.8 M EUR

4.9 M x 4 parcels x 15% fit x 6.5 EUR = 19 M TAM; x 35% corridors x 12% niche = 0.8 M SAM. Freight deregulated: safety fitness certificate for commercial carriers, courier insurance for cars. Calgary-Edmonton (~300 km) is one of the cleanest single lanes in Canada.

  • Watch: Edmonton-Fort McMurray adds length and is a commuter lane with real demand - candidate second lane.

4Launch Waves - Free Markets Only

Wave 1 - Months 0-12: UK, Germany, Netherlands, Florida, Ontario

High SAM combined with the lightest cargo rules. Sequence the corridors: London-Manchester (UK), Berlin-Hamburg (Germany), Miami-Orlando (Florida), Toronto-Ottawa (Ontario), Randstad ring (Netherlands).

Wave 2 - Months 12-24: Australia, Belgium, Poland, India, Nordics, British Columbia, Alberta

Australia's long lanes justify higher contributions; Belgium is frictionless but small; Poland and India add volume; the Nordics round out Europe; BC and Alberta are the smaller Canadian free markets with clean single lanes.

Net conclusion

The free-market universe is smaller than it looks - twelve markets where the cargo lane runs without a licence gate. The two biggest prizes are the UK and Germany. In North America the free entries are Ontario, British Columbia and Alberta (Canada) plus Florida (USA); every other US state and Canadian province requires an authority or permit and is filtered out of this report.

5US and Canada Focus - Florida and Ontario Registration

With the free-market filter, Florida and Ontario are the only unblocked entries in the US and Canada. Both are easy, and both are realistic for a Canadian company - here is exactly what registration means in each.

Florida - registration for a Canadian company

  1. US entity: the easiest path is a US subsidiary (Delaware or Florida LLC) with an IRS EIN (free, issued in days). Alternative: qualify the Canadian corporation directly in Florida through the Florida Division of Corporations - filing fee about 35 USD, plus an annual report (~150 USD/year) and a Florida registered agent (~100-300 USD/year).
  2. Federal broker authority - the real step: USDOT number + MC property-broker authority (~300 USD filing) + BOC-3 process agent + UCR registration + 75,000 USD surety bond, held through the US subsidiary. This is a federal requirement for the cargo platform, not a Florida requirement.
  3. Florida itself adds almost nothing for general freight: intrastate trucking is deregulated - no state operating authority. Drivers using commercial vehicles need USDOT numbers and liability insurance; private cars doing courier work need commercial courier insurance. Avoid household-goods moves at launch (separately regulated by FDACS).
  4. Local basics: county business tax receipt (small fee), sales tax registration where required, US bank account and payment processing.

VERDICT: EASY Florida is one of the cheapest and fastest states for a foreign company: ~35 USD qualification, no intrastate freight licence, and the only heavy item is the same federal FMCSA broker stack you would need in any US state. Budget a few weeks, mostly for the bond and the bank account.

Ontario - registration for a Canadian company

  1. No foreign qualification needed: an Ontario (or federal Canadian) corporation, a CRA business number, and HST registration (courier services are taxable - collect 13% HST).
  2. Freight is deregulated intra-provincially: no operating authority for goods. Vehicles over 4,500 kg need CVOR registration; private cars doing courier work need courier / hire-and-reward insurance - personal policies do not cover it.
  3. City-level business licensing may apply (Toronto and others) - check the municipality before opening a corridor.
  4. Stay intra-provincial at launch: Toronto-Ottawa and Toronto-London lanes. A paid parcel crossing a provincial border triggers the federal extra-provincial layer.

VERDICT: EASIEST OF THE TWO For a Canadian company, Ontario needs no cross-border entity, no authority and no bond - just business registration, HST and proper courier insurance.

British Columbia - registration for a Canadian company

  1. No foreign qualification: register the BC business online through BC Registries (small fee) - a Canadian company just registers, it does not qualify as foreign.
  2. Freight is deregulated intra-provincially: no operating authority. Commercial vehicles need National Safety Code registration; private cars doing courier work need courier / hire-and-reward insurance.
  3. Municipal business licences may apply (Vancouver, Kelowna).
  4. Stay intra-provincial: Vancouver-Kelowna and Vancouver-Kamloops lanes. Vancouver-Calgary crosses into Alberta and triggers the federal extra-provincial layer at launch.

VERDICT: EASY Same free structure as Ontario, with a smaller market (~5.6 M people).

Alberta - registration for a Canadian company

  1. No foreign qualification: register the Alberta business online (small fee).
  2. Freight is deregulated intra-provincially: no operating authority. Commercial carriers need a Safety Fitness Certificate (Alberta Transportation); private cars doing courier work need courier insurance.
  3. Municipal licensing may apply (Calgary, Edmonton).
  4. Best lane: Calgary-Edmonton (~300 km), one of the cleanest single lanes in Canada.

VERDICT: EASY ~4.9 M people - the smallest of the three Canadian free markets, but the Calgary-Edmonton lane is simple and predictable.

Bottom line for the US and Canada question

Under the free-market filter, the unblocked North American entries are Ontario, British Columbia and Alberta (Canada) plus Florida (USA). Ontario is the biggest of the three Canadian markets and the first launch; BC and Alberta are valid second-wave Canadian expansions; Florida stays the single free US entry via the federal broker stack. All of them can be run from one Canadian company.

Canada - is the whole country a free market?

No - Canada is free province by province, not as one country. Two walls stop a national cargo rideshare network:

  1. Quebec is blocked: CTQ carrier and broker permits are required for for-hire goods - the strictest cargo regime in North America. Skip Quebec cargo at launch.
  2. Cross-provincial parcels are not free: carrying a paid parcel across a provincial border is an extra-provincial undertaking under the federal Motor Vehicle Transport Act - it needs a safety fitness certificate and, for trucks, provincially issued extra-provincial authority. A private car doing it sits in a grey zone, so every launch lane must stay inside one province.

What is free: intra-provincial cargo in 9 of 10 provinces - Ontario, British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, PEI and Newfoundland (registration + insurance only; the Territories need verification).

Passenger contrast: the carpool lane is freer than cargo - cost-sharing carpool is legal in all 10 provinces, and interprovincial cost-sharing carpool is treated as private travel, not commercial. Only the cargo lane hits the two walls above.

Play for a BC company going Canada-first

Launch BC intra-provincial (Vancouver-Kelowna, Vancouver-Kamloops), then open Alberta (Calgary-Edmonton) and Ontario (Toronto-Ottawa) as separate intra-provincial operations. Leave Vancouver-Calgary, Toronto-Montreal and anything touching Quebec for after a licensed-carrier structure exists - they are the only non-free parts of the Canadian map.

Is it zero-procedure for a BC incorporation? The honest checklist

No - "free" means no operating authority, not zero procedures. Deregulation removes the licence gate; it does not remove insurance, tax, contract and consumer-law duties. For a BC company running intra-provincial personal-vehicle cargo over a marketplace, the mandatory pre-launch procedure is:

  1. Insurance - the big one: ICBC personal-use policies are void for delivery work. Every driver vehicle must be moved to a business-use rate class with courier / hire-and-reward coverage (ICBC plus optional private excess). Skipping this is the fastest way to lose a claim and expose the driver personally.
  2. Taxes: GST (5%) applies to courier services - register and remit; confirm PST treatment with an accountant.
  3. Municipal licences: Vancouver, Kelowna and other cities license delivery/courier businesses (the inter-municipal business licence scheme helps across municipalities).
  4. National Safety Code: heavier commercial vehicle classes need NSC registration - confirm the exact threshold for the vehicle classes onboarded.
  5. Contracts and law: independent-contractor terms, BC PIPA privacy compliance, BPCPA consumer protection, escrow and refund rules from the operations manual.
  6. Passenger lane: cost-sharing carpool is legal in BC, but only while contributions stay cost-sharing and capped - if a payment starts to look like a fare, the Passenger Transportation Board regime applies.

VERDICT: SAFE WITH A CHECKLIST, NOT "ZERO PROCEDURE" The position is genuinely favorable - no authority, no board approval and no permit for intra-provincial goods - but insurance is a hard requirement, not a detail. Treat the six items above as the mandatory pre-launch procedure.

Who buys the insurance? The central policy design

Yes - a central platform policy is exactly the right answer for occasional carriers, and it is how every gig platform solves this. Drivers should never buy commercial insurance for a trip they make a few times a year.

  1. Platform master policy (blanket, one policy for all carriers): cargo liability per trip plus non-owned auto liability while a trip is active. One policy covers every driver on the platform, bought and held by the company, with the cost recovered inside the platform service fee. Roadie, DoorDash and every TNC run this model.
  2. Driver base policy stays personal, but must allow the platform use: in BC, ICBC is the monopoly base insurer - the personal policy must be switched to the business/delivery rate class, or a claim can be denied; the platform master policy tops up but does not replace that base. Alberta has an approved rideshare-style insurance endorsement that wraps a personal policy. Ontario and the Atlantic provinces use private-market endorsements. This is a per-driver onboarding step, not a policy purchase.
  3. Sender-side per-parcel insurance add-on (2-4% of declared value): stays a product sold at listing time - it covers the sender's loss/damage, and it is separate from the platform master policy (which protects the platform and the driver).
  4. Claims flow: loss or damage on a trip = sender compensated from the add-on policy; driver fault reviewed separately with warnings/limits, never billing the occasional carrier personally - this keeps carriers willing to report honestly.
  5. Partner: work with one insurer across provinces (the carriers that built TNC products in Canada are the natural starting point). Expect the master policy premium to be priced per active trip mile.

BOTTOM LINE The platform owns the insurance stack (master cargo + liability + add-on); the occasional carrier only has to switch the base policy to business use once during onboarding. Nothing to buy per trip.

The "already traveling anyway, under 60 kg, cost-sharing only" argument - what it changes

This is exactly the right product design, and it is legally decisive for the passenger lane - but for the cargo lane the law does not care. Here is precisely what the argument changes and what it does not.

Where the argument mattersWhat it changes
Passenger carpool (BC and all provinces)Decisive. Non-commercial carpool = the driver makes the trip anyway + shares only actual costs + no profit. That is the exact legal test that keeps the passenger lane outside the PTB/TNC regimes. Keep contributions capped to a published cost formula.
Cargo - the legal classificationNothing. Carrying someone else's goods for any payment is for-hire in cargo law - whether the trip would have happened anyway, and whether the contribution covers the travel cost. There is no "cargo cost-sharing" exemption anywhere (in US federal rules the argument fails entirely - FMCSA has no such concept).
Cargo - the practical outcome in Canada's free provincesLittle practical difference. Because intra-provincial freight is deregulated in BC, Alberta, Ontario and the rest, a for-hire classification still requires no operating authority. The obligations stay exactly the six-item checklist: business-use insurance class, GST, municipal licences, contracts. The cost-sharing wording changes nothing in that checklist.
Driver income tax (Canada)Helpful. A contribution at or below actual trip costs means no net business income to tax - keep simple records (fuel, tolls) to prove it.
ICBC base insurance (BC)Nothing. ICBC asks what the vehicle is used for, not whether the driver profits. A car carrying a parcel for a contribution is a delivery use - the business/delivery rate class is still required, or claims can be denied.
Under 60 kg hard capStrongly helpful. It keeps every vehicle far below the commercial-vehicle thresholds (3.5 t, 500 kg payload in Belgium, 2.5 t in Poland), reinforces the trunk-sharing story, avoids CVOR/NSC commercial-vehicle registration in Canada and keeps the EU light-vehicle exemptions intact. Bake the 60 kg cap into the app as a hard limit.

The honest one-liner

Call the product cost-sharing - it is true for passengers and true in practice for pricing - but run the cargo compliance as light for-hire courier in every province: business-use insurance, GST, licences, contracts. The six-item checklist does not shrink because the wording changes; it shrinks only because the province deregulated freight - and BC's has.

6TAM, SAM, SOM - Definitions

  • TAM - Total Addressable Market: the full annual value of intercity small-parcel transport in the country or region that could theoretically move by trunk sharing - every parcel that is small enough, allowed, and paid. A ceiling, not a target.
  • SAM - Serviceable Addressable Market: the slice of TAM this platform can actually serve at launch: parcels inside the chosen anchor corridors, in the urgent / fragile / door-to-door segments that would pay for same-day cost-sharing.
  • SOM - Serviceable Obtainable Market: what a corridor-first launch realistically captures - Year 1 (0.3% of SAM, winning a first corridor) and Year 3 (2% of SAM, several corridors live). These are the planning targets.

7Methodology and Assumptions

TAM = population x intercity small parcels per person per year x share that fits trunk sharing x average contribution SAM = TAM x corridor share (share of the market inside the anchor corridors) x niche share (urgent / fragile / door-to-door segments) SOM = SAM x capture rate (Year 1: 0.3% - one corridor; Year 3: 2% - several corridors) Ranking score = SAM (in million EUR) x (6 - cargo regulation difficulty)

9How to Adjust the Model

  • Change the assumptions: every cell in section 7 is editable. Real parcel-per-capita data for a target country should replace the estimates before a pitch.
  • Add a market: apply the formula - population x parcels x fit share x contribution = TAM; TAM x corridor share x niche share = SAM; SAM x 0.3% and 2% = SOM Year 1 and Year 3. It qualifies as free only if cargo difficulty is 1 or 2.
  • Re-score difficulty: re-check the regulation guide first - cargo difficulty changes whenever a country changes its light-vehicle thresholds.
  • Re-rank: sort by SAM x (6 - difficulty). That is the whole model.
