Six decisions before the budget is final

The award is settled at $40,000, so these are now budget decisions rather than application decisions. Ordered by how much each one can cost you.

01

Instructor wages are probably counted twice

Your Use of Funds paragraph puts academic instructors inside Staffing ($45,000), while line 5, Academic Coaching & Tutoring ($12,000), describes the same instruction. A reviewer will read that as the same wages paid twice.

Pick one home. Either instructors sit in Staffing and line 5 becomes materials, assessment and external tutoring contracts, or instructors sit in line 5 and Staffing means the coordinator and event organizers only.

02

Food stays out of every line

Food and drink is treated as hospitality and is not an eligible gaming expense, even inside a camp. Camps still happen - the venue, the paid leaders, the supplies and the transport are all claimable, the meals are not.

Keep meals in the program budget and fund them from fees, donations or another source. Do not let them reappear in the Use of Funds wording.

03

Gifts stay out - honoraria are not gifts

Gifts cannot be purchased with gaming funds in any amount, so they are not in this plan. An honorarium paid to a named person for delivering a session is a service payment, not a gift, and remains eligible as line 20 - but it is not part of the $40,000 split above.

04

Multi-year equipment: no

You asked whether you may purchase equipment. No - capital purchases are not eligible. Anything with a useful life beyond the grant year (laptops, tablets, furniture, sports equipment, printers) has to be bought with other money.

Consumables are fine: paper, printing, craft supplies, workbooks, single-use program kits.

05

"Youth Sports and Physical Wellness" is not in the narrative

Budget line 7 ($2,000) has no matching activity in the program description. A reviewer reading the two documents side by side will spot it.

Either add the activity to the narrative - what, who, when, where - or remove the line and move the $2,000 to instruction or camps.

06

Mixed staffing needs three separate treatments

You confirmed employees, contractors and volunteers all deliver the program. They cannot share one line:

Employees - payroll, prorated to program time. Contractors - contract plus invoice, same proration. Volunteers - in-kind hours disclosed at $0 cash, or an honorarium only if they are paid for a specific service.

04

Program narrative

Your answers, kept as written, arranged in the order the application asks for them.

What activities and services were delivered?

Over the previous 12 months, the Ubuntu: I Am Because We Are - Youth Academic Mentorship and Leadership Program delivered year-round academic enrichment, mentorship, and leadership programming to youth across Metro Vancouver. The program is open to all youth, with prioritized outreach to youth from immigrant, newcomer, and refugee families who face barriers to academic success, post-secondary access, and community integration.

  • Weekly mentorship circles every Saturday, connecting youth with trained mentors for leadership development, academic support, homework guidance, goal-setting and social-emotional mentorship through group workshops, collaborative learning and individual check-ins.
  • Weekly academic instruction through grade-based math tutoring led by trained instructors: numeracy development, curriculum reinforcement, test preparation and individualized learning plans supported by assessments and progress tracking.
  • Reading and literacy programming year-round through the Future Generation Reading Club for university-bound youth and the Young Seekers Reading Circle for high-school students: reading challenges, essay workshops, discussion groups, feedback sessions and academic recognition.
  • University and career pathway programming: admissions and scholarship seminars, essay-writing clinics, annual Career and University Faculty Fairs, and guided campus discovery tours.
  • Quarterly math enrichment camps: intensive instruction, collaborative problem-solving labs and exam simulations, including CEMC contest preparation and mock exams.
  • Seasonal leadership camps: communication, teamwork, resilience, civic leadership, digital literacy and facilitation skills.
  • Mentor recruitment and training year-round: safeguarding protocols, cultural-responsiveness training, facilitation workshops and leadership development.
  • Community engagement: youth participation in Canada Day community programming and Winter Light Festival initiatives, promoting intercultural exchange, volunteerism and public educational showcases.
  • Monitoring and evaluation embedded across all activities: attendance tracking, literacy and numeracy assessments, participant learning plans, mentor evaluations, feedback surveys and outcome reporting.

Who benefited, and how many participated?

Approximately 125 youth participants through weekly mentorship, tutoring, literacy programming, academic camps, pathway seminars and leadership activities. Programming was supported by approximately 35 trained mentors and volunteers, with an estimated 350 additional community participants engaged through public showcases, campus tours, fairs and community events involving families, educators and community partners.

The majority of youth served were from immigrant, newcomer and refugee communities, while all activities remained fully open and accessible to youth from the broader public.

When and how often did activities take place?

  • Weekly: mentorship circles, math tutoring, reading clubs, CEMC practice groups.
  • Monthly: academic skills workshops and admissions or scholarship seminars.
  • Quarterly: math enrichment camps and exam simulations.
  • Seasonally: leadership development camps and campus tours.
  • Annually: Career and University Faculty Fairs, Canada Day activities, Winter Light Festival programming.

Where were activities delivered?

Community centres, public libraries, rented classrooms and tutoring facilities, university and college campuses for tours and fairs, regional camp venues, and municipal event spaces.

How and by whom was the program delivered?

Program coordinators overseeing scheduling, safeguarding, intake, reporting and evaluation; paid instructional staff delivering tutoring, literacy instruction, camps and workshops; volunteer mentors facilitating weekly mentorship and leadership coaching; guest educators supporting pathway sessions; and community and educational partners providing collaborative venues and support. All mentors and staff completed safeguarding, child-protection and facilitation training prior to service delivery.

How is the program ongoing, and for how many years?

The program has been delivered for one year and is ongoing rather than a one-time project. Weekly mentorship circles, math tutoring and reading clubs continue on the same schedule, and the camps, fairs and campus tours repeat on their annual calendar. The 2025-26 budget is built on that same delivery model, with the same coordinator, instructors and volunteer mentors.

Why is the program needed in the community?

Youth across Metro Vancouver, particularly those from immigrant, newcomer and refugee families, face persistent barriers to academic achievement, post-secondary navigation and community integration: language transitions, limited familiarity with the Canadian school system, reduced access to tutoring or mentorship, and social isolation. Public school resources and settlement services often lack sufficient capacity for individualized academic support and sustained mentorship.

The Ubuntu program addresses these gaps with consistent academic tutoring, literacy development, mentorship relationships, leadership training and pathway navigation that complement formal education and improve long-term educational engagement, confidence and community belonging.

How has the program demonstrated accessibility and inclusivity?

All activities are open to youth from the entire community, with prioritized outreach to immigrant, newcomer and refugee families. Programming is offered at no cost to participants, or with full fee waivers when required. Activities are delivered across transit-accessible community venues, including community centres and libraries. Multilingual communication support is provided for families, and accommodations are made for varying literacy levels, learning styles and physical accessibility needs. Activity designs incorporate culturally responsive facilitation and inclusive learning methodologies.

How is community support demonstrated?

Through volunteer participation, partnerships and institutional collaboration. Trained volunteer mentors from universities and community organizations support weekly programming and special events. The program maintains active partnerships with libraries, community centres, schools and post-secondary institutions that provide venue access, outreach referrals, campus tour coordination and workshop facilitation. Families regularly attend community showcases, university fairs and campus tours. Collaborative engagements with educators and local agencies further validate demand and relevance.

How was the program made available and communicated?

Year-round promotion through partnerships with schools, libraries, settlement organizations, community centres, multicultural networks, social media, community newsletters and targeted digital outreach. Printed flyers and registration information are distributed at partner locations. Information sessions for parents and youth are held during community events and workshops, and referrals are received directly from educators, community workers and settlement agencies.
