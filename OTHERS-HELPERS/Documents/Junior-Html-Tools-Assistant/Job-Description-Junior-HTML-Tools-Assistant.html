The job in one page

One person who turns approved ideas into finished artifacts with AI: complete curriculum packs built in our own Curriculum Builder, and small working HTML tools. She is also the first independent, hostile user of everything we ship.

What she does all day

  • Produce curricula, about 45 percent. Research the official source, structure the course, write every lesson, build the coverage matrix, walk the course as a learner, fill the gaps.
  • Build tools, about 25 percent. Write the spec, generate the tool with AI inside our template, test it until it passes the spec.
  • Break, about 20 percent. Use tools and courses like a confused first-time user, and log real defects with reproduction steps.
  • Check byproducts, about 10 percent. Demo data, screenshots, help text, catalog rows. Generated by AI, verified by her in minutes.

What she never does

  • Existing tool logic or tool rules
  • Architecture, pricing, product claims
  • Git, database, environment files
  • CMS upload or publishing

What stays with you

  • Idea approval and spec approval
  • Line-by-line review of her code
  • Final manual acceptance
  • Upload, publish, release

What you get in 10 weeks

  • 2 complete curriculum packs and 1 approved outline
  • 3 to 5 small tools accepted after your review
  • 1 defect report per week, from week 4
  • Every touched tool left with current screenshots, demo data and help text
  • Supervision: 6 to 8 h/week, then 3 h/week, then 2 h/week
The test that keeps this honest: if an AI can finish a task in one command, it is a byproduct, not an assignment. The junior is paid for judgement, iteration and accountability, not for pressing a button that already works.

1. What changed from the first draft

Revision 1 built the role around work that AI already does. You were right to reject it. This revision keeps those tasks only as byproducts of a real deliverable.

Revision 1 said Reality Revision 2 does instead
Run screenshot capture for 12 tools per week One script She decides whether the screenshots prove the feature, and fixes the state that produces them. Minutes per tool, attached to a tool she built or tested.
Write demo data sets One AI prompt She checks whether the data would be recognised as real by the person who uses that tool, and corrects what AI got generically wrong.
Update 60 catalog rows Trivial A one-minute byproduct when a tool is accepted. Never a standalone assignment.
Fill document templates AI plus script A byproduct of the tool she just built. She checks that no sentence claims something the tool does not do.
Write evidence reports Bureaucracy Cut down to one screen of proof per delivered tool: what passed, what failed, what an owner should check.

The trade-off you are accepting with revision 2. Output and value go up, but so does risk: a tool built by a school-leaver with AI can look correct and be broken in a way nobody sees until a user hits it. That is why this revision adds three things the first draft did not need: a restricted class of tools she may build, a mandatory acceptance protocol, and your line-by-line code review before anything is uploaded.

Revision 3 adds two things. First, curriculum production: complete course packs built in Curriculum Builder, starting with a driving licence theory course. It is the lowest-risk real output in the library, and it teaches the tools by using them. Second, one written delivery loop that both curricula and tools follow, so the work stops depending on your memory of how the last one was done.

2. The three things you asked about, in plain words

Catalog row

One line in the master list of tool ideas. It answers: what is the idea, does it exist yet, how important is it, where does it live, is it published, what is the note for later. It is the inventory line of one tool.

Example of one catalog row
Idea: Personal Tax Preparation. Status: built. Priority: high. Folder: PERSONAL/GENERAL/personal-finance/PersonalTaxPreparation. Published: 6 of 8 subjects. Note: needs screenshots after the new dashboard. Code version: 1.4.2.

Copy in templates

The sentences that sit inside fixed document blocks. The structure is decided in advance; only the words change, and no new fact may be added. Typical slots: three sentences under "What this tool does", three benefit cards, five FAQ answers, one release note row, one social post.

Example slot and fill
Slot: "What this tool does" - three sentences, no adjectives.
Fill: "Collects invoices and expense receipts in one list. Calculates totals per currency. Exports a monthly report as PDF."

Evidence report

The proof that a task was actually done, in a form someone else can check in under a minute. "It works" is not evidence. A command, a path, a count and one opened image is evidence.

Example evidence block for one tool
Command: screenshot capture for InvoiceAndExpenseCollector.
Result: 9 files written to docs/screenshots/, manifest updated.
Proof: 01-dashboard-desktop.png opened and shows the new totals row.
Warning: none.

In revision 2 all three are small. Catalog row: 1 minute. Copy slots: 10 to 20 minutes. Evidence block: 3 minutes. They arrive attached to a tool she built or tested, plus one consolidated weekly list. They are never the week's work.

3. The design rule: AI types, she judges

3.1 What AI removes from the job

AI does these, fast and cheap

  • Typing code and markup
  • First drafts of any text
  • Boilerplate structure
  • Plausible demo data
  • Running a documented script
  • Generating document shells

AI cannot do these, and that is the job

  • Decide whether a screenshot proves the feature
  • Know whether demo data feels real to the actual user
  • Discover how a confused person breaks the tool
  • Be accountable for a delivered artifact
  • Check an AI claim against the running page
  • Choose what is worth building

3.2 The 15-minute rule

No task is assigned if its total human time is under 15 minutes, unless it is a byproduct step inside a larger deliverable. Anything shorter is done by the AI or by you.

3.3 The new definition of junior work

AI produces, she inspects, corrects and signs. That is the whole design. It means her value is not speed of typing; it is suspicion, persistence and the willingness to say "this looks right but it is wrong, and here is how".

Old junior job AI-era junior job What it requires
Produce the artifactOwn the acceptance of the artifactA written definition of done, before the work starts
Follow stepsFollow steps, then attack the resultA fixed attack list of 12 actions
Report progressReport what passed, what failed and what you should checkOne screen of proof, no storytelling
Learn the toolLearn the rules, and learn where AI is confidently wrongA prompt log and a five-bullet explanation of every tool she delivers

3.4 Honest limits of a school-leaver with AI

Limit Consequence for the plan
Cannot judge code qualityEvery file she produces is reviewed by you, line by line, for at least the first three tools.
Trusts a plausible AI answerShe must demonstrate each claim against the running tool before it counts.
Weak domain knowledgeHer research is input, never the final word. You approve the spec before code is generated.
Cannot design a systemShe builds single-purpose tools only. Anything with objects, integrations or multiple users stays with you.

4.1 Recommended combination

Model 5 from week 1, model 3 in parallel, model 2 from week 4. Curriculum production is the safest high-value work in the whole list: no code, an external standard to check against, and a finished asset at the end. While she produces course 1 in weeks 1 to 3, she also runs model 3 acceptance passes on two existing tools, which teaches her the rules of the library. If she passes those twice, she adds tool building from week 4. If she does not, she stays on curricula and testing and still delivers a real asset.

This is the answer to your question about giving her curriculum work: yes, and earlier than tool building, because a wrong lesson is edited while a wrong tool ships. The reverse order costs you more supervision for the same first month.

4.2 Why not simply hire a developer

A developer costs several times more and would not do the repetitive testing and byproduct work at all. The point of this role is not cheap code. It is a second pair of hands with judgement, time and accountability, amplified by AI.

6. The curriculum track and the delivery loop

Yes to curriculum building, and it should be the first real work she does. A course is a finished asset with no code risk, an external standard to check it against, and one clear test: can a learner follow it. It also teaches the tool library by using it, which no amount of reading the rules achieves.

6.1 Why this is the best fit in the library

PropertyConsequence
No codeA weak lesson is edited, not debugged. Nothing in the platform can break.
External standardGood is not a matter of taste. The official syllabus either is covered or is not.
Real outputA course someone can teach from, that the tool can store and hand over.
Teaches the toolsShe learns Curriculum Builder by producing with it, not by reading about it.
AI is strong but insufficientAI drafts lesson text well, knows the official requirements poorly, and cannot tell whether a beginner can follow it. That gap is her job.

6.2 What a curriculum pack contains

PartWhat it must contain
Course briefOne page: who it is for, entry level, duration, what the learner can do at the end, and what the course is not.
Source listEvery official document used, with its version or date. No source, no claim.
StructureSections, then lessons. A lesson title says what the learner will be able to do, not only the topic name.
Lesson contentPer lesson: objective, three to six key points, explanation, example, practice or quiz, and the common mistake.
Coverage matrixOne row per official topic: which lesson covers it, fully or partly. A missing topic is either filled or excluded in writing with a reason.
Learner walk-throughA written record of following the course from the start, with time per lesson and every point of confusion.
Review packWhat an expert must check, plus the open questions. This is what makes expert review take hours instead of weeks.
Handover fileThe curriculum object and its lesson documents in a form you can load into the live tool. She produces it in the local harness; you import it.
Worked example: driving licence theory
Course brief: adult learner preparing for the theory exam, 42 lessons, about 12 hours of study.
Sections: 1 Road rules, 2 Signs and signals, 3 Vehicle and safety, 4 Emergencies and first aid, 5 Sharing the road, 6 Exam preparation.
Coverage matrix: one row per chapter of the official handbook for the chosen jurisdiction.
Every lesson: objective, key points, one worked example, a five-question quiz, the common mistake.
Label on every page: draft for expert review, not official advice.

The jurisdiction is your decision, not hers. The pack cites the official handbook for that jurisdiction and never paraphrases a rule from memory.

6.3 The loop, written once and used for every artifact

This is the procedure you described, made explicit. It applies to a course, a tool, a document or a dataset. The artifact changes; the stages do not.

# Stage Artifact produced Gate to move on
0FrameOne paragraph: what the artifact is, who it is for, and the exit criteria in numbersYou approve the scope and the stop condition
1ResearchSource list plus a two-page note: what the official material requires, what similar products doSources are official, dated and sufficient
2SSOTOne document deciding purpose, structure, fields, inclusions and exclusionsYou approve the SSOT before anything is produced
3Task listAtomic tasks of four hours or less, each with one acceptance line, grouped into releasesEvery task is testable and fits the week
4ApplyThe work itself, one task at a timeEach task is checked as it closes, not at the end
5TestThe acceptance list with pass or fail and evidence, plus the learner walk-through for contentA written result, never an opinion
6Gap listFailures, missing items and confusion points, converted into new tasksNew tasks replace finished ones; nothing is added silently
7RepeatBack to stage 4 until the exit criteria are metMaximum three improvement rounds per week
8FreezeYour acceptance, the byproducts, a version and a handover noteYou sign; the artifact is now referenced, not edited

Seven rules that keep the loop honest

  1. No stage skipped. No content and no code before the SSOT exists.
  2. Every round ends with a written test result. Looks good is not a result.
  3. The stop condition is fixed at stage 0. Without it the loop never ends.
  4. A task without an acceptance line is not a task.
  5. Ideas discovered mid-loop go on the idea list, never into the current artifact.
  6. Three improvement rounds per artifact per week, then either freeze it or park it.
  7. You approve at stage 0, stage 2 and stage 8 only. Everything else is self-service.

6.4 Two variants of the same loop

Content loop

Official source list, coverage matrix, outline, lessons, learner walk-through, gap list, fill, walk again.

Definition of done: coverage is 100 percent, every lesson has the six parts, and a learner finishes the course without help.

Code loop

Spec, task list, build in small generations, five acceptance gates, 12-action attack list, defect list, fix, attack again.

Definition of done: all gates pass, no fatal or major defect open, and the tool does one job clearly.

6.1 Green class, the only tools she may build

Allowed

  • Single purpose, one screen, no login

10.3 Your ten-minute manual pass

  1. Open the harness. Does it load clean with no console error?
  2. Use it as the intended user for two minutes. Is the main job obvious without reading anything?
  3. Empty state: what do you see before entering anything?
  4. Extreme input: one very long text, one zero, one negative.
  5. Reload. Is the state where you left it?
  6. Narrow the window to phone width. Anything unusable?
  7. Read the code for: forbidden API, secrets, external calls, unrequested features.
  8. Read the five-bullet explanation. Would a non-coder understand the behaviour?

11. Hiring and the paid practical test

11.1 Who to look for

  • Suspicious by nature. Looks at a result and asks "what if I do this instead".
  • Honest about not knowing. Cheap to train, expensive to fool.
  • Follows the rule even when the rule feels silly. This is the whole safety model.
  • Comfortable with AI and unimpressed by it. Uses it as a tool, checks it as a suspect.
  • Writes short and clear. The job is mostly written.

11.4 Interview questions

  1. Show me something AI made for you that looked right and was wrong. How did you notice?
  2. How do you decide when AI output is good enough to hand over?
  3. Tell me about a time you broke something and said so.
  4. What would you do if your tool worked for you but not for someone else?
  5. How would you test a calculator you just built?
  6. This job bans git, database access and publishing absolutely. Is that acceptable?
  7. How would you tell me a spec is wrong, before or after building it?
  8. Which part of the practical test was hardest, and what did you try?
  9. What would you want to learn in ten weeks?
  10. Do you prefer building or checking? Why?

11.5 Red flags

  • Delivers something that works in the demo but was never tested outside it.
  • Cannot explain AI output, only paste it.
  • Reports "it works" without a single test.
  • Ignores the file naming or adds extra files.
  • Hides a known failure until asked.
  • Wants creative freedom in the first month.
